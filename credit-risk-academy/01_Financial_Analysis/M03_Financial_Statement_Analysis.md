## 1. Business Purpose

### Why Credit Analysts Read Financial Statements Differently to Accountants

An accountant prepares financial statements to comply with reporting standards. An auditor checks that the statements comply with those standards. A credit analyst reads the same statements to answer one question: **can this borrower repay its debt from cash generated by the business?**

This single purpose transforms how you read every line. Revenue matters not because it demonstrates growth, but because it generates the cash that services interest and principal. Assets matter not because they appear on a balance sheet, but because they either generate cash or provide collateral if the borrower fails. Profit matters almost not at all — a business can be highly profitable and still default if its cash is trapped in working capital or consumed by capital expenditure.

The financial statements are the primary evidential base for every credit decision. A Relationship Manager will tell you the borrower is excellent, has banked with you for 30 years, and has never missed a payment. The credit analyst's job is to verify this with numbers and to identify the risks the borrower may not themselves recognise — working capital pressure as the business grows, covenant breach risk as margins compress, refinancing risk as debt matures. Financial statements are where this investigation starts.

### The Three-Statement System in a Credit Context

The three financial statements — the Profit and Loss Account (P&L), the Balance Sheet, and the Cash Flow Statement — are an interconnected system. Each feeds the others:

- **P&L** records revenues and costs over a period, producing net profit (or loss)
- **Balance Sheet** records assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time; retained profit from the P&L is added to equity each period
- **Cash Flow Statement** reconciles the net profit (an accounting concept) with the actual movement in cash (a real concept), explaining why a profitable business can run out of cash

In credit analysis, the Cash Flow Statement is the most important. But you cannot read it without understanding the P&L that feeds it, and you cannot assess solvency without the Balance Sheet. The statements must be read together.

### Spreading Financials: The Bank's Standard Process

Banks do not work from PDF annual reports. They enter financial data into a **spreading template** — a standardised Excel or proprietary system format — that reclassifies and reformats reported figures into a consistent structure. This process is called **spreading financials** or **financial spreading**.

The spreading template serves several purposes:
1. **Consistency** — every borrower's financials are presented in the same format, enabling peer comparison and portfolio analytics
2. **Credit adjustment** — the template strips out accounting treatments that distort credit analysis (IFRS 16 leases, goodwill, deferred tax)
3. **Covenant tracking** — covenant-defined ratios are calculated directly from the spread template
4. **Trend analysis** — three to five years of spread data sit side by side, enabling trend analysis that is impossible from a single annual report

Every credit analyst in a corporate bank spends a significant portion of their time spreading financials. It is a core competency, not an administrative task.

## 2. Accounting Concepts

### Revenue Recognition (IFRS 15 / ASC 606)

Revenue is recognised when (or as) performance obligations are satisfied — that is, when control of goods or services passes to the customer. For credit analysts, the key risks are:

- **Front-loaded revenue recognition** — long-term contracts where revenue is recognised on percentage-of-completion may inflate current income; if the project fails to complete, prior revenue must be reversed
- **Bill-and-hold arrangements** — revenue recognised before goods are shipped creates accounts receivable without corresponding cash
- **Contract modifications** — changes to long-term contracts can accelerate or defer revenue in ways that distort the trend

**Credit adjustment:** Be sceptical of revenue that has been recognised but not yet collected. High receivables days (see M05) relative to peers is a warning sign. Always cross-reference revenue to operating cash flow — divergence signals aggressive revenue recognition.

### Depreciation and Amortisation

Depreciation (for tangible assets) and amortisation (for intangible assets) are non-cash charges that reduce reported profit without consuming cash. This is why EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation) is a better proxy for cash generation than net profit — it adds back these non-cash charges.

However, depreciation represents real economic consumption of assets. A business that depreciates its plant over 10 years must eventually replace that plant. The cash cost of replacement is capital expenditure. A key credit analytical skill is assessing whether reported depreciation approximates maintenance capex. If depreciation is materially lower than maintenance capex (common in capital-intensive industries), EBITDA overstates sustainable cash generation.

**Accounting policies that matter:**
- Straight-line vs declining balance: the same asset gives different annual charges
- Useful economic life assumptions: aggressive (long life, low annual charge) vs conservative
- Residual value assumptions: high residual value reduces annual charge

### Inventory Valuation (FIFO vs AVCO vs LIFO)

UK and IFRS companies use FIFO (first in, first out) or weighted average cost. US GAAP allows LIFO (last in, first out). In an inflationary environment, FIFO produces higher inventory valuations and lower cost of goods sold (higher margins) compared to LIFO. When comparing international borrowers, ensure inventory policies are consistent.

For credit analysis, inflated inventory is a warning sign — it may represent slow-moving or obsolete stock that cannot be converted to cash. Always ask: does inventory growth outpace revenue growth? Is the write-down policy conservative?

### Lease Accounting Under IFRS 16

IFRS 16, effective from January 2019, requires lessees to recognise a **right-of-use (ROU) asset** and a corresponding **lease liability** on the balance sheet for almost all leases (previously, operating leases were off-balance sheet).

**Impact on credit analysis:**

| Metric | Pre-IFRS 16 | Post-IFRS 16 |
|--------|-------------|--------------|
| EBITDA | Lower (rent expense reduces EBITDA) | Higher (rent replaced by depreciation on ROU asset, which is added back to EBITDA) |
| Total Debt | Lower (lease liability off B/S) | Higher (lease liability now on B/S) |
| Net Debt/EBITDA | Denominator smaller, numerator smaller | Both inflate — net effect depends on balance |
| Interest Cover | Higher (no interest on leases) | Lower (lease interest is now visible) |
| DSCR | Higher | Lower (lease principal payments are debt service) |

The critical credit adjustment: many banks calculate ratios on a **pre-IFRS 16 basis** (i.e., reversing the standard) to maintain comparability with pre-2019 data and with US borrowers who remain on ASC 842 with different treatment. When reading a credit paper, always identify whether ratios are stated pre- or post-IFRS 16.

**Adjustment mechanics:**
- Remove ROU asset from fixed assets
- Remove lease liability from debt
- Add back depreciation on ROU asset to get pre-IFRS 16 EBITDA
- Add back lease interest to get pre-IFRS 16 EBIT
- Add back lease payments to operating cash flow (they appear in financing cash flows post-IFRS 16)

### Goodwill and Intangible Assets

Goodwill arises when a company acquires another business for more than the fair value of its net identifiable assets. Under IFRS, goodwill is not amortised but is tested annually for impairment. Under US GAAP, companies may elect simplified goodwill impairment testing.

**Credit implications:**
1. **Large goodwill signals acquisition risk** — the company paid a premium for another business; if that business underperforms, a goodwill impairment charge hits the P&L and reduces equity
2. **Goodwill is not a real asset** — in a liquidation, goodwill has zero realisable value; credit analysts exclude it from Tangible Net Worth (TNW)
3. **Impairment testing assumptions** — discount rates, growth rates, and terminal value assumptions in impairment models are management estimates; in a deteriorating business, these assumptions may be optimistic, with impairment charges materialising too late to provide early warning

**Other intangibles:** Customer relationships, brand names, software, patents — all recognised on acquisition under IFRS 3. Like goodwill, these have limited or no liquidation value and should be excluded from TNW.

### Deferred Tax

Deferred tax arises from temporary timing differences between accounting profit and taxable profit. A deferred tax liability means the company will pay more tax in future periods; a deferred tax asset means it will pay less.

**Credit relevance:** Large deferred tax assets (common after significant losses) should be treated with scepticism — they are only realisable if the company makes sufficient future profits to utilise them. In a stressed scenario, a deferred tax asset may need to be written down, reducing equity and potentially breaching covenants.

### Audit Quality and Audit Opinions

The credit analyst must assess the reliability of financial statements before spreading them:

- **Audit firm:** Big 4 (Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG) provides higher quality assurance than mid-tier firms; unaudited management accounts carry significant risk
- **Audit opinion type:**
  - *Unmodified (clean)*: auditor agrees the statements give a true and fair view
  - *Emphasis of Matter*: clean opinion, but auditor draws attention to a specific issue (e.g., going concern, material uncertainty)
  - *Qualified*: statements are true and fair **except** for a specific matter — this is a red flag
  - *Adverse*: statements do not give a true and fair view — rare but a major red flag
  - *Disclaimer of Opinion*: auditor was unable to obtain sufficient evidence to form an opinion

Any deviation from a clean opinion must be documented in the credit paper and its credit implications assessed.

## 3. Financial Concepts

### The Three Statements: Linkages

The mathematical linkages between the three statements are fundamental to spreading financials correctly. Any error in spreading breaks these linkages, providing automatic error detection.

**P&L to Balance Sheet:**
- Opening Retained Earnings + Net Profit – Dividends = Closing Retained Earnings
- Closing Retained Earnings appears in the equity section of the Balance Sheet

**P&L to Cash Flow Statement:**
- Net profit is the starting point of the indirect method Cash Flow Statement
- Non-cash items (depreciation, amortisation, impairment, share-based payments) are added back
- Working capital movements adjust for the difference between accruals-based profit and cash

**Balance Sheet to Cash Flow Statement:**
- The movement in cash and cash equivalents on the Cash Flow Statement must equal the movement in cash on the Balance Sheet
- Working capital movements on the Cash Flow Statement = changes in current assets/liabilities on the Balance Sheet

**Auto-reconciliation check (must build into every spreading template):**
1. Balance sheet balances: Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity
2. Retained earnings reconciliation: Opening RE + PAT – Dividends = Closing RE
3. Cash movement: Closing cash – Opening cash = Net cash flow per Cash Flow Statement

If any of these checks fail, there is an error in the spreading — find it before proceeding.

### EBITDA: Definition and Credit Relevance

EBITDA = Revenue – Cost of Sales – Operating Expenses + Depreciation + Amortisation

Or equivalently: EBIT + D&A

EBITDA is the most widely used metric in corporate credit because:
1. It is a proxy for operating cash generation before tax, capex, and financing costs
2. It is capital structure neutral (before interest) — allows comparison across differently leveraged businesses
3. It forms the denominator in Net Debt/EBITDA, the primary leverage covenant

**What EBITDA does NOT capture:**
- Tax payments (real cash outflow)
- Capital expenditure (real cash outflow)
- Working capital movements (can be substantial)
- Exceptional items (management may try to exclude recurring costs)

This is why the credit analyst must always build the full cash flow waterfall from EBITDA down to free cash flow.

### Normalisation of Financial Statements

Normalisation is the process of adjusting reported financials to reflect the **ongoing, sustainable economic reality** of the business, removing one-off, non-recurring, or distorting items.

**Common normalisation adjustments:**

| Item | Typical Adjustment |
|------|--------------------|
| Exceptional restructuring costs | Add back to EBITDA if genuinely non-recurring |
| Gain/loss on disposal of assets | Remove from P&L; often below EBIT line anyway |
| Goodwill impairment | Add back to EBITDA (non-cash); note the strategic concern separately |
| Owner/director salary above market rate (private companies) | Reduce to market salary; add back excess to EBITDA |
| Owner/director salary below market rate | Add notional market salary as a cost |
| Related-party transactions at non-market prices | Adjust revenue/costs to arm's length equivalent |
| COVID grants / government subsidies | Note separately; may or may not normalise depending on purpose |
| Uncrystallised insurance proceeds | Only include when received (cash basis) |
| Share-based payment charges | Non-cash; add back to EBITDA for cash flow analysis |

**Critical discipline:** Normalisation is a judgement call that must be defensible. Never normalise a cost as "exceptional" without a clear, documented reason. Companies have a natural incentive to present their numbers in the best light; the analyst's job is to see through this. Recurring "exceptional" items are not exceptional — they are a business characteristic.

### Consolidated vs Subsidiary Accounts

For group borrowers, you must decide which accounts to spread:

**Consolidated accounts** (the whole group):
- Appropriate when the borrower is the holding company and the group is the primary economic unit
- Show the full picture of revenue, assets, and cash generation
- Remove intercompany transactions (eliminates internal profits, loans, and dividends)

**Subsidiary accounts** (a specific entity within the group):
- Appropriate when the borrower is a subsidiary (ring-fenced asset, project finance)
- Show the standalone economic performance of the borrowing entity
- Risk: management fees, intercompany dividends, or cash sweeps can strip cash from the subsidiary

**Rule of thumb:** Always obtain both if possible. If the holding company is a shell with no trading activity, the consolidated accounts are the only useful set. If the borrower is a subsidiary that generates the cash while the holdco has debt, you need both — and you need to understand the inter-company cash flows.

## 4. Statistical Concepts

### Time Series Analysis of Financial Statements

A single year of financial statements tells you very little. Three years is the minimum; five years is better. The credit analyst is looking for:

- **Trends in key metrics:** Is revenue growing, stable, or declining? Is EBITDA margin expanding or compressing? Is leverage increasing or decreasing?
- **Volatility:** How much do EBITDA and cash flow vary year to year? High volatility increases default risk because the downside scenario may be much worse than the average
- **Mean reversion:** Are strong years followed by weak years? This is common in cyclical industries (commodities, shipping, construction)

**Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR):**

CAGR = (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/n) – 1

Use CAGR to summarise revenue and EBITDA trends over the analysis period. A 3-year revenue CAGR of 8% with an EBITDA margin stable at 15% is a very different credit story from a 3-year CAGR of 8% achieved by acquiring lower-margin businesses (margin dilution risk).

### Variance Analysis

Year-on-year variance analysis decomposes the change in a metric into its components:

- **Volume effect:** more units sold at the same price
- **Price effect:** same units sold at higher prices
- **Mix effect:** sales shifted towards higher or lower margin products
- **Cost effect:** input cost changes

For credit analysis, this matters because:
- Revenue growth driven by price increases is more sustainable than volume growth in saturated markets
- Margin compression driven by input cost inflation may be temporary; driven by competitive pricing pressure, it may be permanent
- Understanding the source of variance helps you assess whether the budget/forecast is credible

### Correlation and Co-movement

When analysing a business with multiple revenue streams or geographic segments, assess whether they are correlated:

- **Positively correlated segments** offer no diversification benefit — they will all decline together in a downturn
- **Negatively correlated segments** provide genuine diversification — a retailer with both discretionary and non-discretionary products may be more resilient than pure discretionary

This matters for stress testing: if all revenue streams deteriorate simultaneously, what is the impact on DSCR?

## 5. Regulatory Framework

### Basel III / CRR2 Capital Requirements and Financial Statement Data

Banks' regulatory capital requirements depend on the Probability of Default (PD) assigned to each borrower. For IRB-rated (Internal Ratings Based) banks, PD models are trained on historical financial data — the ratios extracted from spread financials feed directly into PD models. Accurate spreading is therefore a regulatory requirement, not just an analytical tool.

### BCBS 239 — Risk Data Aggregation Principles

The Basel Committee's Principles for Effective Risk Data Aggregation (BCBS 239) require banks to maintain accurate, timely, and consistent risk data. Spread financials are a primary risk data input. Banks must be able to demonstrate data lineage — that the number in the credit system traces back to the audited source document.

**Practical implication:** When spreading, always record the source document (company name, annual report year, page number), the date of spreading, and the spreader's name. Any adjustments (normalisation, IFRS 16 reversal) must be documented with rationale.

### FCA / PRA Supervisory Expectations

The PRA's supervisory expectations for credit risk (SS11/13, updated through the Capital Requirements Regulation) require banks to conduct thorough credit analysis including:
- Review of at least two years of audited financials (three preferred)
- Documented assessment of business sustainability and repayment capacity
- Identification and stress testing of key financial risks

The EBA Guidelines on loan origination and monitoring (EBA/GL/2020/06) require banks to assess:
- Debt service capacity based on sustainable cash generation
- Balance sheet strength and asset quality
- Quality of financial information (audit opinion, accounting policies)

## 6. Data Required

### Minimum Data Requirements for Credit Analysis

**Primary documents:**
- Audited consolidated financial statements (3 years minimum, 5 preferred)
- Notes to financial statements (critical — detail on debt maturity, covenant headroom, contingent liabilities, pension obligations, segment reporting)
- Auditor's report (for opinion type)
- Management accounts (latest period if annual report is >6 months old)
- Cash flow forecast / budget (from borrower)

**Supplementary documents:**
- Directors' Report and Strategic Report (qualitative context)
- Group structure chart
- Intercompany loan agreements (for group borrowers)
- Existing facility agreements (for covenant definitions)
- Asset register (for asset-backed lending)

### Notes to Financial Statements: What to Look For

The notes are not optional reading — they contain information that directly affects credit analysis:

| Note | Credit Relevance |
|------|-----------------|
| Accounting policies | Identify aggressive policies; assess IFRS 16 detail |
| Segmental reporting | Identify which segments drive EBITDA; assess diversification |
| Debt maturity schedule | Refinancing risk; how much debt matures in the facility tenor |
| Covenant details | Existing covenant terms; headroom to breach |
| Related-party transactions | Identify cash leakage; management fee structures |
| Contingent liabilities | Tax disputes, litigation — potential hidden liabilities |
| Pension obligations | Defined benefit deficit; can be a significant off-balance sheet liability |
| Post-balance sheet events | Material events between year-end and signing date |
| Going concern | Any doubts raised by management or auditor |

## 7. How Analysts Actually Work

### The Spreading Process: Step by Step

**Step 1: Obtain and review the source documents**
Download the annual report. Read the Strategic Report and Directors' Report first — they provide narrative context that helps you understand what you are about to spread. Note the audit opinion immediately. Flag any qualifications.

**Step 2: Open the spreading template**
Every bank has its own template, but they all follow the same logic. You will have separate tabs for: P&L, Balance Sheet, Cash Flow Statement, and Ratios. Some banks use proprietary systems (Moody's CreditLens, S&P Global Market Intelligence, Finastra Fusion Credit). The analytical logic is the same regardless of the tool.

**Step 3: Spread the P&L**
Start with revenue. Map the company's reported line items to the template categories. This is never a one-to-one mapping — a manufacturing company may report "Cost of materials," "Staff costs," "Other operating costs" where your template has "Cost of Sales" and "Operating Expenses." You must apply judgement in the allocation, and document it.

Enter figures as reported first. Then apply normalisations, clearly separated so that both reported and normalised figures are visible.

**Step 4: Spread the Balance Sheet**
Map reported balance sheet line items to the template. Key allocations: Which line is reported as "Trade and other receivables" but contains prepayments (current assets) and long-term deposits (non-current)? Read the notes. Get it right.

Check: Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity. If this does not balance, you have made an error.

**Step 5: Spread the Cash Flow Statement**
Map reported cash flows to the template. Identify lease principal payments (post-IFRS 16 they appear in financing activities — note this for your DSCR calculation).

Check: Net movement in cash = Closing cash – Opening cash. Cross-reference to the Balance Sheet.

**Step 6: Run reconciliation checks**
The three auto-checks (balance sheet balances, RE reconciliation, cash movement) must all pass. If they do not, find the error. Do not proceed until they pass.

**Step 7: Calculate ratios**
With a clean, balanced spread, ratios calculate automatically from formulas.

**Step 8: Normalise and document**
Apply normalisation adjustments with clear documentation. For each adjustment, state: what is being adjusted, why, and by how much.

**Step 9: Compare to prior year**
Year-on-year movement analysis. Flag any items that require further investigation or explanation from the borrower.

### Practical Tips from the Desk

- **Always read the note on accounting policies first** — it tells you which accounting choices have been made and alerts you to areas where the numbers may not be comparable to peers
- **Reconcile EBITDA yourself** — never take management's stated EBITDA at face value; calculate it from reported figures and compare
- **Watch out for restatements** — if the prior year comparatives have changed between two annual reports, the company has restated. Find out why.
- **Management accounts are not the same as audited accounts** — they may exclude accruals, use different cost allocations, and be prepared to present the best possible picture
- **Ask about seasonality** — a June year-end for a retailer means the balance sheet is taken at a high-inventory, high-payables point; a December year-end means the post-Christmas low point

### Conditional Formatting for Error Detection

Apply these conditional formats to the check cells:
- Balance Check: Green fill if "BALANCED", Red fill if "ERROR"
- RE Recon Check: Green if "OK", Red if "ERROR"
- Cash Recon Check: Green if "OK", Red if "ERROR"

Use `=COUNTIF(CheckRange,"ERROR")>0` in a summary cell to create a dashboard warning if any check fails.

## 9. SQL Implementation

### Database Schema for Spread Financials

```sql
-- Core entity tables
CREATE TABLE companies (
    company_id      INT PRIMARY KEY IDENTITY(1,1),
    company_name    NVARCHAR(200) NOT NULL,
    registration_no NVARCHAR(50),
    sector          NVARCHAR(100),
    country         NVARCHAR(100),
    currency        CHAR(3) DEFAULT 'GBP',
    created_date    DATETIME2 DEFAULT GETDATE()
);

CREATE TABLE financial_periods (
    period_id       INT PRIMARY KEY IDENTITY(1,1),
    company_id      INT REFERENCES companies(company_id),
    period_end      DATE NOT NULL,
    period_type     CHAR(6) CHECK (period_type IN ('ANNUAL','INTERIM')),
    audit_opinion   NVARCHAR(50),  -- CLEAN, EMPHASIS, QUALIFIED, ADVERSE
    auditor_firm    NVARCHAR(100),
    spread_date     DATE,
    spread_by       NVARCHAR(100),
    source_document NVARCHAR(500),
    ifrs16_applied  BIT DEFAULT 1,
    UNIQUE (company_id, period_end)
);

CREATE TABLE income_statement (
    is_id               INT PRIMARY KEY IDENTITY(1,1),
    period_id           INT REFERENCES financial_periods(period_id),
    revenue             DECIMAL(18,3),  -- £millions
    cost_of_sales       DECIMAL(18,3),
    gross_profit        AS (revenue - cost_of_sales) PERSISTED,
    operating_expenses  DECIMAL(18,3),
    ebitda_reported     AS (revenue - cost_of_sales - operating_expenses) PERSISTED,
    depreciation        DECIMAL(18,3),
    amortisation        DECIMAL(18,3),
    ebit                AS (revenue - cost_of_sales - operating_expenses
                            - depreciation - amortisation) PERSISTED,
    interest_expense    DECIMAL(18,3),
    interest_income     DECIMAL(18,3),
    pbt                 AS (revenue - cost_of_sales - operating_expenses
                            - depreciation - amortisation
                            + interest_income - interest_expense) PERSISTED,
    tax_charge          DECIMAL(18,3),
    pat                 AS (revenue - cost_of_sales - operating_expenses
                            - depreciation - amortisation
                            + interest_income - interest_expense
                            - tax_charge) PERSISTED,
    dividends           DECIMAL(18,3),
    -- Normalisation adjustments
    adj_exceptional     DECIMAL(18,3) DEFAULT 0,
    adj_owner_salary    DECIMAL(18,3) DEFAULT 0,
    adj_related_party   DECIMAL(18,3) DEFAULT 0,
    adj_ifrs16_rent     DECIMAL(18,3) DEFAULT 0,  -- pre-IFRS 16 adjustment
    ebitda_normalised   AS (revenue - cost_of_sales - operating_expenses
                            + adj_exceptional + adj_owner_salary 
                            + adj_related_party) PERSISTED,
    ebitda_pre_ifrs16   AS (revenue - cost_of_sales - operating_expenses
                            + adj_exceptional + adj_owner_salary 
                            + adj_related_party - adj_ifrs16_rent) PERSISTED
);

CREATE TABLE balance_sheet (
    bs_id                   INT PRIMARY KEY IDENTITY(1,1),
    period_id               INT REFERENCES financial_periods(period_id),
    -- Fixed assets
    ppe_net                 DECIMAL(18,3),
    rou_assets              DECIMAL(18,3),  -- IFRS 16
    goodwill                DECIMAL(18,3),
    other_intangibles       DECIMAL(18,3),
    investments             DECIMAL(18,3),
    deferred_tax_asset      DECIMAL(18,3),
    -- Current assets
    inventory               DECIMAL(18,3),
    trade_receivables       DECIMAL(18,3),
    other_receivables       DECIMAL(18,3),
    cash                    DECIMAL(18,3),
    -- Current liabilities
    trade_payables          DECIMAL(18,3),
    other_payables          DECIMAL(18,3),
    st_borrowings           DECIMAL(18,3),
    current_lease_liab      DECIMAL(18,3),
    -- Long-term liabilities
    lt_borrowings           DECIMAL(18,3),
    lt_lease_liab           DECIMAL(18,3),
    pension_deficit         DECIMAL(18,3),
    deferred_tax_liab       DECIMAL(18,3),
    -- Equity
    share_capital           DECIMAL(18,3),
    retained_earnings       DECIMAL(18,3),
    other_reserves          DECIMAL(18,3)
);

-- Query: Year-on-year changes for a borrower
SELECT 
    fp.period_end,
    fp.audit_opinion,
    is_.revenue,
    LAG(is_.revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY fp.company_id ORDER BY fp.period_end) AS prior_revenue,
    ROUND(
        (is_.revenue - LAG(is_.revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY fp.company_id ORDER BY fp.period_end))
        / NULLIF(LAG(is_.revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY fp.company_id ORDER BY fp.period_end), 0) * 100
    , 1) AS revenue_growth_pct,
    is_.ebitda_normalised,
    ROUND(is_.ebitda_normalised / NULLIF(is_.revenue, 0) * 100, 1) AS ebitda_margin_pct,
    bs.goodwill,
    ROUND(bs.goodwill / NULLIF(bs.share_capital + bs.retained_earnings + bs.other_reserves, 0) * 100, 1) AS goodwill_pct_equity
FROM financial_periods fp
JOIN income_statement is_ ON fp.period_id = is_.period_id
JOIN balance_sheet bs ON fp.period_id = bs.period_id
JOIN companies c ON fp.company_id = c.company_id
WHERE c.company_name = 'Tesco PLC'
ORDER BY fp.period_end;

-- Query: Detect companies with qualified audit opinions in the portfolio
SELECT 
    c.company_name,
    fp.period_end,
    fp.audit_opinion,
    fp.auditor_firm,
    is_.revenue,
    is_.ebitda_normalised
FROM companies c
JOIN financial_periods fp ON c.company_id = fp.company_id
JOIN income_statement is_ ON fp.period_id = is_.period_id
WHERE fp.audit_opinion IN ('QUALIFIED', 'ADVERSE', 'DISCLAIMER')
  AND fp.period_end >= DATEADD(YEAR, -2, GETDATE())
ORDER BY fp.audit_opinion, c.company_name;

-- Query: Retained earnings reconciliation check (data quality)
SELECT 
    c.company_name,
    fp.period_end,
    bs.retained_earnings AS re_balance_sheet,
    prev_bs.retained_earnings + is_.pat - is_.dividends AS re_calculated,
    ABS(bs.retained_earnings - (prev_bs.retained_earnings + is_.pat - is_.dividends)) AS discrepancy
FROM financial_periods fp
JOIN companies c ON fp.company_id = c.company_id
JOIN balance_sheet bs ON fp.period_id = bs.period_id
JOIN income_statement is_ ON fp.period_id = is_.period_id
JOIN financial_periods prev_fp ON prev_fp.company_id = fp.company_id
    AND prev_fp.period_end = (
        SELECT MAX(fp2.period_end) 
        FROM financial_periods fp2 
        WHERE fp2.company_id = fp.company_id 
          AND fp2.period_end < fp.period_end
    )
JOIN balance_sheet prev_bs ON prev_fp.period_id = prev_bs.period_id
HAVING ABS(bs.retained_earnings - (prev_bs.retained_earnings + is_.pat - is_.dividends)) > 0.5
ORDER BY discrepancy DESC;
```

```python
    depreciation: float

    adj_ifrs16_rent: float = 0.0   # Subtract to get pre-IFRS 16 EBITDA

    def gross_profit(self):
        return self.revenue - self.cost_of_sales

    @property
    def gross_margin(self):
        return self.gross_profit / self.revenue if self.revenue else np.nan

    def ebitda_pre_ifrs16(self):
        return self.ebitda_normalised - self.adj_ifrs16_rent

    def ebit(self):
        return self.ebitda_reported - self.depreciation - self.amortisation

    def net_interest(self):
        return self.interest_income - self.interest_expense

    @property
    def pbt(self):
        return self.ebit + self.net_interest

                "RE Reconciliation": re_check,

        return pd.DataFrame(results)

        rows = []
```

## 11. Interview Questions

**Q1: What are the three financial statements and how do they link?**
The P&L shows revenues and costs over a period, producing net profit. The Balance Sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. The Cash Flow Statement reconciles net profit to actual cash movement. The key links: net profit feeds into retained earnings on the Balance Sheet; the movement in cash on the Cash Flow Statement equals the change in cash on the Balance Sheet; working capital movements reconcile the accruals-based P&L to actual cash flows.

**Q2: What is EBITDA and why do credit analysts focus on it?**
EBITDA is Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation. Credit analysts focus on it because it is a proxy for operating cash generation before financing costs and capital expenditure decisions — making it comparable across companies with different capital structures and depreciation policies. It is the denominator in the Net Debt/EBITDA leverage ratio, which is the primary covenant in most corporate lending facilities.

**Q3: What is normalisation and when would you normalise a cost out of EBITDA?**
Normalisation removes one-off, non-recurring items to present a sustainable run-rate EBITDA. You normalise a cost out of EBITDA when it is genuinely non-recurring and non-operational — for example, a one-off restructuring charge for a factory closure. You do NOT normalise costs that, while labelled exceptional, recur regularly (a company that has restructuring charges every year has an operational problem, not exceptional costs). Each adjustment requires explicit documentation and justification.

**Q4: How does IFRS 16 affect credit analysis?**
IFRS 16 brings operating leases onto the balance sheet, inflating both EBITDA (rent expense is replaced by depreciation and interest) and total debt (lease liabilities are now on-balance sheet). This makes post-IFRS 16 ratios incomparable to pre-2019 data. Many banks adjust ratios back to a pre-IFRS 16 basis by reversing the lease capitalisation. The analyst must always identify whether ratios are stated pre- or post-IFRS 16.

**Q5: Why is goodwill a credit concern?**
Goodwill represents the premium paid for acquired businesses. It is not realisable in a liquidation (zero recovery value). If the acquired business underperforms, goodwill is impaired — this hits the P&L and reduces equity, potentially breaching equity or TNW covenants. Banks therefore calculate Tangible Net Worth (TNW = equity minus goodwill and other intangibles) for covenant purposes and view high goodwill as a latent risk.

**Q6: A company has a qualified audit opinion. How does this affect your credit analysis?**
A qualified opinion means the auditor believes the financial statements give a true and fair view EXCEPT for a specific matter. I would first identify the nature of the qualification — is it a scope limitation (auditor could not obtain evidence) or a disagreement (auditor disagrees with accounting treatment)? I would then assess the materiality of the qualified item. I would document the qualification in the credit paper, consider whether it affects key ratios or covenants, and may apply a haircut to stated figures or seek additional security. Depending on severity, a qualified opinion might be a condition subsequent to facility drawdown — the borrower must provide clean accounts within a specified period.

## 12. Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Spreading from management accounts without flagging it**
Management accounts are not audited. They may exclude accruals, use optimistic cost allocations, and present the business in its best light. Always clearly label the source (audited / management / unaudited). Apply a credit overlay — management accounts are indicative only.

**Mistake 2: Not reading the notes**
The financial statements without the notes are incomplete. Debt maturity, pension deficits, contingent liabilities, and segment profitability are all in the notes. An analyst who spreads the primary statements but does not read the notes will miss major risks.

**Mistake 3: Normalising recurring exceptional items**
Companies sometimes repeatedly label similar costs as "exceptional." An analyst who normalises these each year is effectively ignoring a real business cost. Three or more years of consecutive "exceptional" restructuring costs are an ongoing business reality and should not be adjusted out.

**Mistake 4: Forgetting IFRS 16 and comparing 2020 to 2023 leverage ratios without adjustment**
The introduction of IFRS 16 in 2019 means that pre- and post-2019 leverage ratios are not directly comparable for any company with significant leases (retailers, airlines, restaurants are particularly affected). Always check when IFRS 16 was adopted and flag the discontinuity.

**Mistake 5: Ignoring the audit opinion**
Many junior analysts default to spreading financial statements without checking the audit opinion. A qualified or adverse opinion is a material credit risk — the numbers you are spreading may not be reliable.

**Mistake 6: Using consolidated accounts when the borrower is a subsidiary**
If the loan is to a specific subsidiary and cash is swept up to the parent, the consolidated accounts show cash generation that is not available to service the subsidiary's debt. Always use the borrowing entity's accounts as the primary analysis.

## 13. Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Tesco PLC — IFRS 16 Impact on Leverage

Tesco is one of the UK's largest retailers with a substantial leased property estate. The introduction of IFRS 16 dramatically increased both its reported EBITDA and its reported debt.

**Before IFRS 16 (FY2019 illustrative basis):** Rental charges of approximately £1.9 billion per year ran through the P&L as operating expenses, reducing EBITDA. No lease liabilities appeared on the balance sheet.

**After IFRS 16 (FY2023):** The right-of-use assets and lease liabilities of approximately £8.95 billion and £9.1 billion respectively appear on the balance sheet. EBITDA is approximately £1.9 billion higher (the rent is no longer in operating expenses) but reported net debt is approximately £9.1 billion higher.

**Credit lesson:** Tesco's Net Debt/EBITDA on a post-IFRS 16 basis appears higher than on a pre-IFRS 16 basis in absolute terms, but the economic leverage (ability to service all financial obligations including leases) is what matters. A banker who compares Tesco's 2023 leverage ratio to its 2018 ratio without adjusting for IFRS 16 will draw incorrect conclusions about whether leverage has improved or deteriorated.

### Case Study 2: Goodwill Impairment Risk — A General Illustration

A UK building materials company acquires a European competitor for £500 million, recognising £300 million of goodwill (60% of the purchase price). The acquisition thesis relies on synergies that prove difficult to achieve, and the acquired business faces margin pressure from rising raw material costs. Two years later, the impairment test (using management's discounted cash flow model) concludes the goodwill is impaired by £120 million.

The £120 million impairment charge hits the P&L, reduces PAT and retained earnings, and reduces total equity. The TNW covenant (minimum TNW of £200 million) is now breached. The bank must decide: waive, require remedy, or enforce.

**Credit lesson:** Large goodwill balances create covenant risk even when the underlying business continues to generate cash. The impairment does not affect cash flow but does affect balance sheet ratios. Always stress-test TNW covenants against a plausible impairment scenario at the point of credit approval.

### Week 4: Sector Application
- Repeat the spreading exercise with a different sector borrower (e.g., a manufacturing company available via Companies House).
- Compare the spreading challenges across sectors — which line items are easy to map? Which require judgement?

### Ongoing Practice
- Spread one new annual report each month.
- After 6 months, you will have built a personal library of 6+ spreads. Compare them across sectors — this is where credit intuition develops.
