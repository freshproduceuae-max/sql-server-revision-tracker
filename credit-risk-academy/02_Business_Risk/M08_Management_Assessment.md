## 1. Business Purpose

An experienced credit analyst will tell you that the majority of defaults they have lived through were ultimately management failures. The financials were there — the leverage was manageable, the industry was sound, the business model was viable. What caused the default was a management team that made poor capital allocation decisions, failed to respond to market changes, misrepresented financial information to the bank, or simply ran out of personal energy and competence when the business faced its first serious stress event.

Management assessment is the credit analyst's attempt to evaluate the human element of credit risk: whether the people running this business have the competence, integrity, and resilience to manage it through its lifecycle — including through a downturn — and specifically whether they will behave well towards the bank as a creditor.

This is the least quantifiable of all the business risk factors, which makes it the most susceptible to analyst bias and the most requiring of structured discipline. It is also the factor that sophisticated credit professionals rate as most important for predicting outcomes in mid-market corporate lending, where management and ownership are often the same people and there is no deep institutional governance structure to provide a backstop.

**Why management quality is a first-order credit factor:**

The three canonical failure modes in management-driven defaults are:

1. **Competence failure:** Management made strategic errors (over-expansion, ill-timed acquisition, failure to adapt to competitive change) that destroyed value. The business was viable; the management made it unviable.

2. **Integrity failure:** Management concealed information from the bank, manipulated financial information, or used company assets for personal benefit. By the time the bank discovered the true position, the company was already insolvent.

3. **Resilience failure:** Management performed well in benign conditions but lacked the hardness to make the difficult decisions (cost cuts, asset disposals, renegotiation with suppliers) required in a downturn. They delayed, prevaricated, and hoped conditions would improve until it was too late.

The management assessment process is designed to surface early evidence of each failure mode before the credit is approved and money is lent.

## 2. Accounting Concepts

Management quality has direct accounting implications. The financial statements are prepared by management; the quality of those statements reflects management quality.

**Audit opinion quality:**
A clean (unqualified) audit opinion does not guarantee financial statement accuracy, but a qualified, adverse, or "disclaimer of opinion" audit report is a serious red flag. Qualifications relate to specific areas of uncertainty or disagreement between management and auditor. Common qualifications in mid-market company accounts include: going concern uncertainty, inability to verify specific balances (often related-party transactions), disagreement on inventory or provisions policy, and disagreement on revenue recognition. A credit analyst must read the auditor's report, not just the financials.

**Auditor changes:**
An unexplained change of auditor — particularly a switch from a reputable mid-tier firm (BDO, Grant Thornton, Mazars) to a smaller, less well-known practice — is a red flag. Auditors resign when they are unable to obtain sufficient evidence to support an opinion, when they identify integrity concerns about management, or when management pressures them to accept accounting treatments they disagree with. The credit analyst should ask management directly: "Why did you change auditors?" and assess whether the answer is plausible.

**Management accounts quality:**
Most mid-market corporate borrowers provide management accounts to their bank on a monthly or quarterly basis as a covenant condition. The quality of these accounts reveals a great deal about management competence:
- Are they produced promptly (within 10 business days of month end) or habitually late?
- Are they in a consistent format over time, or do they change layout frequently (making period-on-period comparison difficult)?
- Do they include explanatory commentary on variances versus budget?
- Do the management account revenues and EBITDA reconcile approximately to the eventual audited accounts (allowing for year-end adjustments)? Large, unexplained divergences between management accounts and audited accounts are a significant integrity concern.
- Are accruals and prepayments applied consistently? Management can inflate short-term management account profitability by deferring cost accruals, and the divergence only becomes apparent at audit.

**Related-party transactions:**
IFRS requires disclosure of related-party transactions — transactions between the company and its directors, major shareholders, or entities connected to them. These transactions are a primary fraud vector and a common governance weakness in private mid-market companies. Examples:

- Management fees paid by the operating company to a holding company or family trust owned by the director — a mechanism for extracting cash from the business in a tax-efficient manner, but also a mechanism for extracting value from the bank's collateral
- Sales to connected entities at below-market prices (transfer pricing) — understates the profitability of the main operating company
- Loans from the company to directors (director's loan accounts) — can represent undisclosed equity extraction; large director loan balances are a warning sign
- Property owned by the director leased to the operating company at above-market rents — value transferred to the director at the expense of the operating company's profitability

The credit analyst must read the related-party disclosure note in the audited accounts and understand every transaction listed. Any related-party transaction that is not clearly at arm's length, explained, and commercially justified is a red flag.

## 3. Financial Concepts

**Key-man risk and its financial impact:**

Key-man risk is the dependency of a business's financial performance on one or more specific individuals — typically a founder, CEO, or key technical/commercial specialist. If that individual becomes unavailable (death, illness, departure), financial performance deteriorates.

The financial signatures of key-man dependency include:
- Revenue concentrated in relationships personally owned by the founder (particularly relevant in professional services, consulting, financial services)
- Business development pipeline dependent on the founder's network
- Technical knowledge concentrated in one individual (engineering companies where the chief engineer holds all the intellectual knowledge)
- Customer retention linked to individual relationships rather than institutional relationships

The credit implication: key-man risk is not a reason to refuse credit, but it is a reason to: (a) require key-man life and critical illness insurance, with the bank as beneficiary or loss payee, (b) size the debt at a level sustainable through a disruption period, (c) include a key-man departure event in the covenant package as an early warning trigger.

**Track record through prior downturns — the analytical framework:**

The most valuable data point on management quality is their behaviour and decision-making during prior periods of financial stress — economic downturns, sector-specific crises, or company-specific stress events. Questions to ask:

- Did the company trade through 2020 (COVID) without government support? Or was CBILS/BBLS the reason it survived?
- In 2022 (cost inflation, energy price spike), did the management proactively reprice contracts and manage costs, or did they absorb margin compression and hope it would reverse?
- Have any of the directors been associated with previous company insolvencies? (Companies House director search; Insolvency Service disqualification register)
- Have the current owners/directors taken this business through a full economic cycle? A management team that has only operated in benign conditions has unproven resilience.

**Free cash flow generation as a management quality indicator:**

Management quality shows in the cash flow statement. Companies managed by competent, cash-focused management consistently convert EBITDA to free cash flow efficiently. Companies with management problems often show an EBITDA-to-cash-flow gap that is large and growing — attributable to working capital mismanagement, excessive capex, or cash extraction through related-party transactions.

The ratio **Free Cash Flow / EBITDA** (also called "cash conversion") is a useful management quality proxy. A consistently cash-converting business (FCF/EBITDA > 60%) suggests disciplined working capital management and controlled capex. An erratic or declining cash conversion suggests management problems — either operational (cannot collect receivables, builds excess inventory) or governance (cash is leaving through non-obvious channels).

**Management equity stake and alignment of interests:**

A management team that owns a significant equity stake in the business has aligned interests with the bank to a degree: both the manager and the bank want the business to succeed. The credit implication of high management equity ownership is positive for alignment but creates a concentration of personal wealth risk — if the business fails, the manager may lose everything, creating a perverse incentive to take excessive risks to try to recover ("gamble for resurrection" in the academic literature).

Management teams with no equity stake have different incentives — they may not fight hard enough to protect the bank's position if a turnaround is personally painful. Management teams with very high leverage on their own equity may be tempted to gamble. The ideal from a credit perspective is a management team with meaningful but not all-encompassing personal stake, and with personal guarantees on the debt that align their incentive to maximise value for creditors.

## 4. Statistical Concepts

**Management quality as a predictor of default — empirical evidence:**

Empirical credit research has consistently found that qualitative management factors are incrementally predictive of default, even after controlling for financial ratios. Altman and Sabato (2007) find that qualitative variables including management characteristics improve the discriminatory power of SME credit models. Berg et al. (2020) using fintech loan data demonstrate that non-traditional information signals (analogous to management quality indicators) have significant predictive value.

Practically, banks that track management quality scores in their rating models can demonstrate empirically that:
- Borrowers with low management quality scores (poor management) have higher default rates than their financial ratios alone would predict
- Management quality score deterioration (year-on-year decline) is a leading indicator of default, typically appearing 12–24 months before financial distress becomes visible in audited accounts
- The interaction effect is important: poor management quality amplifies the credit risk from high leverage (poor management + high leverage is far more dangerous than either alone)

**Behavioural scoring in credit — the psychology of transparency:**

Transparency with the bank — how forthcoming, timely, and accurate is management's communication? — is a behavioural credit variable. Banks that track transparency events (late management accounts, covenant waiver requests, information requests that go unanswered, meetings that are cancelled) can build a "transparency score" that is predictive of subsequent deterioration.

Research in banking (Brunner, Krahnen, Weber, 2000) shows that relationship managers' qualitative assessments of borrower behaviour — including communication quality and transparency — have predictive value for default independent of financial signals. This supports the practice of incorporating transparency metrics into business risk scores.

**Bayesian updating of management quality assessment:**

Credit analysts should think of management assessment as a Bayesian process: they begin with a prior belief about management quality based on initial evidence, then update that belief as new evidence arrives. The prior is formed from:
- Background research (Companies House, previous defaults, reference checks)
- First management meeting (credentials, presentation, knowledge of their business)
- Quality of initial information provided

The prior is then updated as evidence arrives over the relationship:
- Quality and timeliness of management accounts
- Covenant compliance behaviour
- Communication during stress events
- Accuracy of forecasts versus actuals

A management team that consistently demonstrates high-quality, timely, proactive communication should have their management quality score improved over time. One that is consistently late, evasive, or inaccurate should have it deteriorated — even if no specific crisis has occurred.

## 5. Regulatory Framework

**FCA Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SMCR) — financial services borrowers:**

For borrowers that are themselves regulated financial services firms, the FCA's Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SMCR) provides a framework for management accountability. Under SMCR, senior managers must be approved by the FCA and carry personal liability for failures in their areas of responsibility. A borrower that is an FCA-regulated firm (IFA, asset manager, broker) faces personal management accountability risk — if the FCA revokes an approval, the individual cannot perform their function, and if that individual is the key-man, the business may be unable to operate. Credit analysts lending to regulated financial services firms must understand SMCR and check that key individuals are on the FCA Register.

**PRA CP6/22 and credit risk model requirements:**

The PRA's Credit Risk Modelling consultation papers require banks to demonstrate that their internal rating models are adequately validated, including testing whether the qualitative factors (management assessment being the primary one) are independently predictive. Banks must show calibration evidence that management quality scores as assigned in practice correspond to observed default rates.

**Company directors' duties — UK Companies Act 2006:**

Directors of UK companies have statutory duties under the Companies Act 2006, including: to act in accordance with the company's constitution, to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members, to exercise independent judgement, to exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence, to avoid conflicts of interest, and to declare interests in proposed transactions. A director who has been disqualified under the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 is prohibited from acting as a director for a specified period (typically 2–15 years). Credit analysts must verify that no director is disqualified before approving credit; lending to a company with a disqualified director is a regulatory and legal risk.

**The Insolvency Act 1986 — wrongful trading implications:**

Under Section 214 of the Insolvency Act 1986, a director can be personally liable for wrongful trading if they continued to operate a company and incur debts when they knew (or ought to have known) that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvent liquidation. Credit analysts should be aware that when a company in difficulty continues to borrow from the bank, the directors are making a judgement that recovery is possible. If that judgement is wrong and the company subsequently fails, the directors may face personal liability. Understanding the wrongful trading risk helps analysts understand why some directors become very cautious about additional borrowing when a business is under stress — and why the bank's own credit approval team must be equally cautious in such situations.

**GDPR and obtaining information:**

Credit analysts gather personal information about directors (addresses, previous directorships, credit history) during due diligence. This activity must comply with UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. Banks must have a lawful basis for processing personal data (legitimate interest is the most common basis in commercial credit contexts) and must notify borrowers of data processing. This is increasingly relevant as the volume of data sources used for management due diligence (social media, news databases, credit reference agencies) expands.

## 6. Data Required

**Primary data — from the borrower:**

- **Directors' CVs:** Formal career histories, qualifications, and relevant experience. For new customers or large facilities, banks typically request these explicitly.
- **Organisational chart:** Who manages which function? What reporting lines exist? Is there a finance function, or does the MD do everything? Does the business have a capable CFO or only a bookkeeper?
- **Three years of management accounts:** Allows assessment of management accounts quality, consistency, and accuracy relative to audited accounts.
- **Budget and five-year business plan:** Reveals management's planning capability and ambition. An overoptimistic business plan (hockey stick revenue growth with no substantiation) is a management quality red flag.
- **Details of any litigation, regulatory investigations, or disputes:** Must be disclosed in the credit application; non-disclosure is an integrity red flag.

**Secondary data — from public sources:**

- **Companies House — Director search:** Lists all current and historic directorships. Reveals if directors are associated with dissolved or insolvent companies. Free public data.
- **Insolvency Service — Disqualified Directors Register:** Public register of disqualified directors. Must be checked for every director before credit approval. Free public data: https://www.insolvency.gov.uk/disqualified-directors/
- **FCA Register:** Lists approved persons and firms. Must be checked for any borrower in a regulated activity. Free public data.
- **HMRC CCJ Register / Registry Trust:** County Court Judgements registered against directors personally or against the company. Accessible through credit reference agencies.
- **LinkedIn:** Verifies career history stated on CV; identifies professional network and public profile. Also useful for verifying team depth — does the company have a senior finance team, or is there no one with financial qualifications at senior level?
- **Glassdoor / Indeed:** Employee reviews. A pattern of poor management reviews is a qualitative signal, not definitive but worth noting.
- **News and press monitoring:** Factiva, Nexis, Google News — searches for adverse coverage of the company or its directors. Include directors' names and previous company names in the search.
- **Sector-specific databases:** Construction companies — Glenigan contract win data; healthcare — CQC inspection reports; legal services — SRA regulatory database.

**Data from the bank's own systems:**

- **Existing covenant compliance record:** Has the company previously breached covenants? Were waivers requested proactively or reactively?
- **Communication log:** How responsive has management been to bank requests? Are management account submissions typically on time or late?
- **Facility utilisation history:** Patterns of revolving facility usage can reveal cash flow management and any cash flow stress events.
- **Past credit actions:** Were there previous amendments, waivers, or restructuring events? What was management's behaviour during those events?

## 7. How Analysts Actually Work

**The management meeting — structure and what to look for:**

The management meeting is the primary method of management assessment. A well-structured meeting lasts 2–3 hours and follows a sequence designed to elicit meaningful qualitative information, not just a management presentation.

**Preparation (before the meeting):**
- Review the financial statements and identify 3–5 specific anomalies or questions (e.g., "Working capital increased £3m last year despite falling revenue — can you explain that?" or "Your gross margin improved 2% in 2023 while your materials costs appear to have risen — what drove that?")
- Run a Companies House director search on all directors attending
- Check the Insolvency Service disqualification register
- Search the news for all director names and company name
- Prepare the management meeting agenda and share in advance

**Opening (first 20 minutes):**
The Relationship Manager typically opens with relationship context. The credit analyst observes: Does management arrive prepared? Did they read the agenda? Are the right people in the room — is the CFO present if financial questions are on the agenda? One red flag: a managing director who insists on attending alone, without financial colleagues, when financial questions were pre-notified. This may indicate a desire to control information flow.

**Business review (45–60 minutes):**
Ask management to walk you through the business — not the financial statements, but the business: customers, products/services, competitive position, strategy. Effective questions:
- "Who are your top three customers by revenue, and what is each relationship based on?" — tests customer knowledge and reveals concentration
- "Where is your business in three years?" — tests strategic thinking and realism
- "What was the hardest thing about 2022 for your business, and how did you handle it?" — tests resilience and honesty about challenges
- "What keeps you awake at night about the business?" — a management team that says "nothing" is either naive or not being honest; a management team that identifies specific, real risks and explains their mitigation is demonstrating good management

**Financial discussion (30–45 minutes):**
The credit analyst leads this section. Present the anomalies identified in preparation and ask management to explain them. Watch for: fluency (do they know the answer immediately?) versus delay (do they need to check with the accountant?), consistency (does the explanation match what the audited accounts say?), and comfort level (do they handle financial challenge confidently?).

Ask specifically about: forecasting accuracy (compare last year's budget to last year's actuals), working capital management, capex plans, dividend policy, and any plans for acquisitions or disposal.

**Governance and ownership discussion (15–20 minutes):**
Ask about: ownership structure and any plans for ownership change, succession planning, key person dependencies, and related-party transactions. This section often reveals significant information. Well-governed businesses have clear, well-documented answers to these questions. Poorly-governed businesses show confusion, evasion, or the sense that no one has thought carefully about these issues.

**Documentation:**
After the meeting, the credit analyst writes up a management meeting note — typically 500–800 words covering: who attended, topics covered, key management messages, specific risk signals observed, and a proposed management quality score. This note becomes part of the credit file and is reviewed by the Senior Credit Officer.

## 9. SQL Implementation

```sql
-- ============================================================
-- MANAGEMENT ASSESSMENT: Storage, Tracking, and Early Warning
-- Database: SQL Server (T-SQL syntax)
-- ============================================================

-- TABLE DEFINITIONS
-- management_assessments: top-level assessment per borrower per period
-- management_factor_scores: individual factor scores
-- management_events: tracks specific management events over the relationship
-- director_register: tracks directors associated with each borrower

-- ============================================================
-- TABLE CREATION (run once)
-- ============================================================

CREATE TABLE management_assessments (
    assessment_id         INT IDENTITY(1,1) PRIMARY KEY,
    borrower_id           INT NOT NULL,
    assessment_date       DATE NOT NULL,
    analyst_id            INT NOT NULL,
    meeting_date          DATE,
    meeting_attendees     NVARCHAR(500),
    total_mqa_score       DECIMAL(4,2),     -- 1.00-10.00
    mqa_band              VARCHAR(20),
    key_man_risk_score    DECIMAL(3,1),
    related_party_risk    VARCHAR(20),      -- None/Low/Moderate/High/Critical
    transparency_score    DECIMAL(3,1),
    key_risks_narrative   NVARCHAR(3000),
    conditions_imposed    NVARCHAR(1000),
    keyman_insurance_req  BIT DEFAULT 0,
    prior_insolvency_flag BIT DEFAULT 0,
    created_date          DATETIME DEFAULT GETDATE()
);

CREATE TABLE management_events (
    event_id              INT IDENTITY(1,1) PRIMARY KEY,
    borrower_id           INT NOT NULL,
    event_date            DATE NOT NULL,
    event_type            VARCHAR(100),  
    -- Types: 'MgmtAccountsLate', 'CovenantWaiver', 'DirectorChange',
    --        'InformationRefusal', 'MeetingCancelled', 'AuditorChange',
    --        'RPTIdentified', 'PressAdverse', 'CCJRegistered'
    severity              VARCHAR(20),   -- Low/Medium/High/Critical
    description           NVARCHAR(1000),
    analyst_id            INT,
    requires_escalation   BIT DEFAULT 0,
    escalated_date        DATE,
    resolved_date         DATE,
    created_date          DATETIME DEFAULT GETDATE()
);

CREATE TABLE director_register (
    director_id           INT IDENTITY(1,1) PRIMARY KEY,
    borrower_id           INT NOT NULL,
    director_name         VARCHAR(200),
    role_title            VARCHAR(100),
    date_of_appointment   DATE,
    date_of_resignation   DATE,
    prior_insolvencies    INT DEFAULT 0,
    disqualified          BIT DEFAULT 0,
    disqualification_end  DATE,
    ch_reference          VARCHAR(20),  -- Companies House reference
    created_date          DATETIME DEFAULT GETDATE()
);

-- ============================================================
-- QUERY 1: Management Quality Score Migration
-- Detects deteriorating management quality — leading indicator
-- of credit deterioration typically 12-24 months ahead
-- ============================================================

WITH mqa_history AS (
    SELECT
        ma.borrower_id,
        b.borrower_name,
        ma.assessment_date,
        ma.total_mqa_score,
        ma.mqa_band,
        ma.key_man_risk_score,
        ma.transparency_score,
        ma.keyman_insurance_req,
        ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
            PARTITION BY ma.borrower_id 
            ORDER BY ma.assessment_date DESC
        ) AS assessment_rank
    FROM management_assessments ma
    INNER JOIN borrowers b ON ma.borrower_id = b.borrower_id
),
current_vs_prior AS (
    SELECT
        curr.borrower_id,
        curr.borrower_name,
        curr.total_mqa_score                    AS current_score,
        curr.mqa_band                           AS current_band,
        curr.transparency_score                 AS current_transparency,
        curr.key_man_risk_score                 AS current_keyman_risk,
        prev.total_mqa_score                    AS prior_score,
        prev.mqa_band                           AS prior_band,
        curr.assessment_date                    AS current_date,
        prev.assessment_date                    AS prior_date
    FROM mqa_history curr
    LEFT JOIN mqa_history prev
        ON curr.borrower_id = prev.borrower_id
        AND prev.assessment_rank = 2
    WHERE curr.assessment_rank = 1
)

SELECT
    borrower_name,
    current_date,
    ROUND(current_score, 2)                     AS current_mqa_score,
    current_band,
    prior_date,
    ROUND(prior_score, 2)                       AS prior_mqa_score,
    prior_band,
    ROUND(current_score - prior_score, 2)       AS score_change,
    current_transparency,
    current_keyman_risk,
    CASE
        WHEN (current_score - prior_score) > 2.0 
            THEN 'CRITICAL DETERIORATION — immediate escalation'
        WHEN (current_score - prior_score) > 1.0 
            THEN 'SIGNIFICANT DETERIORATION — Senior Credit review'
        WHEN (current_score - prior_score) > 0.5 
            THEN 'MODERATE DETERIORATION — enhanced monitoring'
        WHEN (current_score - prior_score) < -0.5 
            THEN 'IMPROVEMENT'
        ELSE 'STABLE'
    END                                         AS migration_flag,
    -- Cross-reference with business risk migration (M07)
    CASE
        WHEN (current_score - prior_score) > 1.0 
            AND current_score >= 6.0
            THEN 'Consider IFRS 9 Stage 2 transfer'
        ELSE NULL
    END                                         AS ifrs9_consideration
FROM current_vs_prior
ORDER BY score_change DESC;

-- ============================================================
-- QUERY 2: Management Event Early Warning Monitor
-- Aggregates adverse management events over rolling 12 months
-- to identify deteriorating management behaviour patterns
-- ============================================================

WITH event_counts AS (
    SELECT
        me.borrower_id,
        b.borrower_name,
        -- Count by event type in last 12 months
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.event_type = 'MgmtAccountsLate' 
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -12, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS late_mgmt_accounts_count,
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.event_type = 'CovenantWaiver'
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -12, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS covenant_waiver_count,
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.event_type = 'DirectorChange'
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -12, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS director_changes_count,
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.event_type = 'AuditorChange'
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -24, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS auditor_changes_24m,
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.event_type = 'RPTIdentified'
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -24, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS rpt_events_24m,
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.event_type = 'InformationRefusal'
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -12, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS info_refusals_count,
        SUM(CASE WHEN me.severity IN ('High', 'Critical')
                 AND me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -12, GETDATE())
                 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)              AS high_severity_events,
        -- Total events
        COUNT(CASE WHEN me.event_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -12, GETDATE())
                   THEN 1 END)                   AS total_events_12m
    FROM management_events me
    INNER JOIN borrowers b ON me.borrower_id = b.borrower_id
    GROUP BY me.borrower_id, b.borrower_name
),
event_scoring AS (
    SELECT
        *,
        -- Composite early warning score
        (late_mgmt_accounts_count * 2) +
        (covenant_waiver_count * 3) +
        (director_changes_count * 2) +
        (auditor_changes_24m * 5) +    -- Auditor change is a serious flag
        (rpt_events_24m * 4) +
        (info_refusals_count * 5) +    -- Information refusal is critical
        (high_severity_events * 3)     AS ew_composite_score
    FROM event_counts
)

SELECT
    borrower_name,
    late_mgmt_accounts_count,
    covenant_waiver_count,
    director_changes_count,
    auditor_changes_24m,
    rpt_events_24m,
    info_refusals_count,
    high_severity_events,
    total_events_12m,
    ew_composite_score,
    CASE
        WHEN ew_composite_score >= 20 THEN 'CRITICAL — immediate review'
        WHEN ew_composite_score >= 12 THEN 'HIGH — Senior Credit Officer'
        WHEN ew_composite_score >= 6  THEN 'MEDIUM — unscheduled review'
        WHEN ew_composite_score >= 2  THEN 'LOW — note on file'
        ELSE 'CLEAR'
    END                                 AS ew_alert_level
FROM event_scoring
WHERE total_events_12m > 0
ORDER BY ew_composite_score DESC;

-- ============================================================
-- QUERY 3: Director Background Check Report
-- Summary of directors with prior insolvency or disqualification
-- concerns — must be reviewed before credit approval
-- ============================================================

SELECT
    b.borrower_name,
    dr.director_name,
    dr.role_title,
    dr.date_of_appointment,
    dr.prior_insolvencies,
    dr.disqualified,
    dr.disqualification_end,
    l.outstanding_balance / 1000000.0   AS exposure_gbp_m,
    CASE
        WHEN dr.disqualified = 1 
            THEN 'DO NOT LEND — disqualified director, legal prohibition'
        WHEN dr.prior_insolvencies >= 2 
            THEN 'HIGH CONCERN — multiple prior insolvencies; 
                  full explanation required'
        WHEN dr.prior_insolvencies = 1 
            THEN 'NOTE — one prior insolvency; obtain explanation and 
                  assess circumstances'
        ELSE 'CLEAR'
    END                                 AS director_risk_flag
FROM director_register dr
INNER JOIN borrowers b ON dr.borrower_id = b.borrower_id
LEFT JOIN (
    SELECT borrower_id, SUM(outstanding_balance) AS outstanding_balance
    FROM loans
    WHERE outstanding_balance > 0
    GROUP BY borrower_id
) l ON b.borrower_id = l.borrower_id
WHERE dr.date_of_resignation IS NULL  -- Current directors only
    AND (dr.prior_insolvencies > 0 OR dr.disqualified = 1)
ORDER BY dr.disqualified DESC, dr.prior_insolvencies DESC;

-- ============================================================
-- QUERY 4: Transparency Score Trend — Management Accounts
-- Tracks management account submission timeliness over time
-- to build a data-driven transparency score
-- ============================================================

WITH ma_submissions AS (
    -- This assumes a table: mgmt_account_submissions
    -- (borrower_id, period_end_date, due_date, submission_date)
    SELECT
        mas.borrower_id,
        b.borrower_name,
        mas.period_end_date,
        mas.due_date,
        mas.submission_date,
        DATEDIFF(DAY, mas.due_date, mas.submission_date) AS days_late,
        CASE
            WHEN mas.submission_date <= mas.due_date THEN 'On Time'
            WHEN DATEDIFF(DAY, mas.due_date, mas.submission_date) <= 5 
                THEN '1-5 Days Late'
            WHEN DATEDIFF(DAY, mas.due_date, mas.submission_date) <= 10 
                THEN '6-10 Days Late'
            ELSE '>10 Days Late (serious)'
        END AS submission_status
    FROM mgmt_account_submissions mas
    INNER JOIN borrowers b ON mas.borrower_id = b.borrower_id
    WHERE mas.period_end_date >= DATEADD(YEAR, -2, GETDATE())
)

SELECT
    borrower_name,
    COUNT(*)                                AS total_submissions,
    SUM(CASE WHEN days_late <= 0 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS on_time_count,
    ROUND(
        SUM(CASE WHEN days_late <= 0 THEN 1.0 ELSE 0 END) * 100.0 / 
        COUNT(*), 1
    )                                       AS on_time_pct,
    ROUND(AVG(CAST(days_late AS FLOAT)), 1) AS avg_days_late,
    MAX(days_late)                          AS worst_delay_days,
    SUM(CASE WHEN days_late > 10 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS serious_late_count,
    -- Derived transparency score (maps to Factor 4.1)
    CASE
        WHEN AVG(CAST(days_late AS FLOAT)) <= 0 AND MAX(days_late) <= 2 
            THEN 1.5  -- Excellent
        WHEN AVG(CAST(days_late AS FLOAT)) <= 2 
            THEN 3.0  -- Good
        WHEN AVG(CAST(days_late AS FLOAT)) <= 5 
            THEN 5.5  -- Average
        WHEN AVG(CAST(days_late AS FLOAT)) <= 10 
            THEN 7.5  -- Poor
        ELSE 9.0      -- Unacceptable
    END                                     AS derived_transparency_score
FROM ma_submissions
GROUP BY borrower_id, borrower_name
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 4  -- Minimum 4 submissions for meaningful analysis
ORDER BY avg_days_late DESC;
```

```python
@dataclass
class MQAFactor:
    """Single factor in the Management Quality Assessment."""
    code: str
    description: str
    section: str
    weight: float
    raw_score: float
    evidence: str = ""
    
    @property
    def weighted_score(self) -> float:
        return self.weight * self.raw_score
    
    def validate(self):
        if not 1.0 <= self.raw_score <= 10.0:
            raise ValueError(f"{self.code}: score {self.raw_score} out of bounds")
        if not 0.0 < self.weight <= 0.5:

class ManagementEarlyWarningDetector:
    """
    Analyses a log of management events to detect deteriorating 
    management behaviour patterns.
    
    Scores and flags borrowers based on the frequency and severity
    of adverse management events.
    """
    
    EVENT_WEIGHTS = {
        'MgmtAccountsLate': 2,
        'CovenantWaiver': 3,
        'DirectorChange': 2,
        'AuditorChange': 5,           # Most serious governance signal
        'RPTIdentified': 4,
        'InformationRefusal': 5,      # Integrity signal
        'MeetingCancelled': 2,
        'PressAdverse': 3,
        'CCJRegistered': 4,
        'LateFilingCompaniesHouse': 1,
    }
    
    ALERT_THRESHOLDS = {
        'CRITICAL': 20,
        'HIGH': 12,
        'MEDIUM': 6,
        'LOW': 2,
    }
    
    def __init__(self, events_df: pd.DataFrame):
        """
        Args:
            events_df: DataFrame with columns: borrower_id, borrower_name,
                      event_date (datetime), event_type, severity
        """
        self.events = events_df.copy()
        self.events['event_date'] = pd.to_datetime(self.events['event_date'])
    
    def compute_ew_scores(self, lookback_months: int = 12) -> pd.DataFrame:
        """
        Computes early warning composite score per borrower.
        
        Returns ranked DataFrame of borrowers with alert levels.
        """
        cutoff = datetime.now() - timedelta(days=lookback_months * 30)
        recent_events = self.events[self.events['event_date'] >= cutoff]
        
        # Count events by type per borrower
        event_counts = recent_events.groupby(
            ['borrower_id', 'borrower_name', 'event_type']
        ).size().reset_index(name='count')
        
        # Apply weights
        event_counts['weighted_count'] = event_counts.apply(
            lambda row: row['count'] * self.EVENT_WEIGHTS.get(row['event_type'], 1),
            axis=1
        )
        
        # Aggregate to borrower level
        summary = event_counts.groupby(
            ['borrower_id', 'borrower_name']
        ).agg(
            total_events=('count', 'sum'),
            ew_score=('weighted_count', 'sum')
        ).reset_index()
        
        # Add most concerning event type
        dominant_event = event_counts.sort_values(
            'weighted_count', ascending=False
        ).groupby('borrower_id').first()[['event_type']].rename(
            columns={'event_type': 'primary_concern'}
        )
        summary = summary.merge(dominant_event, on='borrower_id', how='left')
        
        # Alert level
        def get_alert(score):
            if score >= self.ALERT_THRESHOLDS['CRITICAL']:
                return 'CRITICAL'
            elif score >= self.ALERT_THRESHOLDS['HIGH']:
                return 'HIGH'
            elif score >= self.ALERT_THRESHOLDS['MEDIUM']:
                return 'MEDIUM'
            elif score >= self.ALERT_THRESHOLDS['LOW']:
                return 'LOW'
            return 'CLEAR'
        
        summary['alert_level'] = summary['ew_score'].apply(get_alert)
        summary = summary.sort_values('ew_score', ascending=False)
        
        return summary
    
    def event_timeline(self, borrower_id: int, 
                       borrower_name: str) -> plt.Figure:
        """
        Plots management event timeline for a single borrower.
        Shows event types and severity over time.
        """
        borrower_events = self.events[
            self.events['borrower_id'] == borrower_id
        ].sort_values('event_date')
        
        if borrower_events.empty:
            fig, ax = plt.subplots()
            ax.text(0.5, 0.5, 'No events recorded', ha='center')
            return fig
        
        severity_map = {'Low': 1, 'Medium': 2, 'High': 3, 'Critical': 4}
        severity_colours = {
            'Low': 'green', 'Medium': 'gold', 
            'High': 'orange', 'Critical': 'red'
        }
        
        fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(14, 5))
        
        for _, event in borrower_events.iterrows():
            severity = event.get('severity', 'Medium')
            colour = severity_colours.get(severity, 'grey')
            size = severity_map.get(severity, 2) * 50
            
            ax.scatter(
                event['event_date'], 
                severity_map.get(severity, 2),
                s=size, c=colour, alpha=0.7, zorder=3
            )
            ax.annotate(
                event['event_type'],
                (event['event_date'], severity_map.get(severity, 2)),
                textcoords='offset points', xytext=(0, 10),
                ha='center', fontsize=7, rotation=45
            )
        
        ax.set_yticks([1, 2, 3, 4])
        ax.set_yticklabels(['Low', 'Medium', 'High', 'Critical'])
        ax.set_xlabel('Date')
        ax.set_ylabel('Event Severity')
        ax.set_title(
            f'Management Event Timeline — {borrower_name}',
            fontweight='bold'
        )
        ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
        
        plt.tight_layout()
        return fig

class ForecastAccuracyAnalyser:
    """
    Compares management's budget/forecast to actual results 
    over multiple periods. Systematic over-optimism is a 
    management quality red flag.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, forecast_vs_actual: pd.DataFrame):
        """
        Args:
            forecast_vs_actual: DataFrame with columns:
                borrower_name, period, metric, forecast_value, actual_value
                metrics typically: Revenue, EBITDA, Operating_Cashflow
        """
        self.data = forecast_vs_actual.copy()
        self.data['variance_pct'] = (
            (self.data['actual_value'] - self.data['forecast_value']) /
            self.data['forecast_value'].abs() * 100
        )
        self.data['overoptimistic'] = self.data['variance_pct'] < -10
    
    def accuracy_summary(self) -> pd.DataFrame:
        """Summarises forecast accuracy by borrower and metric."""
        summary = self.data.groupby(['borrower_name', 'metric']).agg(
            periods_assessed=('period', 'count'),
            avg_variance_pct=('variance_pct', 'mean'),
            worst_miss_pct=('variance_pct', 'min'),
            overoptimistic_count=('overoptimistic', 'sum')
        ).reset_index()
        
        summary['overoptimistic_rate'] = (
            summary['overoptimistic_count'] / summary['periods_assessed'] * 100
        )
        
        # Derive transparency score component from accuracy
        def accuracy_to_score(avg_var, overopt_rate):
            if avg_var >= -5 and overopt_rate <= 10:
                return 1.5  # Excellent accuracy
            elif avg_var >= -10 and overopt_rate <= 25:
                return 3.0  # Good
            elif avg_var >= -20 and overopt_rate <= 40:
                return 5.5  # Acceptable
            elif avg_var >= -30:
                return 7.5  # Poor
            else:
                return 9.0  # Chronic over-optimism
        
        summary['derived_factor_4_3_score'] = summary.apply(
            lambda r: accuracy_to_score(
                r['avg_variance_pct'], r['overoptimistic_rate']
            ), axis=1
        )
        
        return summary.round(1)
    
    def plot_accuracy(self, borrower_name: str) -> plt.Figure:
        """
        Plots forecast vs actual over time for a single borrower.
        """
        borrower_data = self.data[
            self.data['borrower_name'] == borrower_name
        ]
        
        metrics = borrower_data['metric'].unique()
        fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, len(metrics), figsize=(5 * len(metrics), 5))
        if len(metrics) == 1:
            axes = [axes]
        
        fig.suptitle(
            f'Forecast vs Actual — {borrower_name}',
            fontweight='bold'
        )
        
        for ax, metric in zip(axes, metrics):
            metric_data = borrower_data[
                borrower_data['metric'] == metric
            ].sort_values('period')
            
            x = range(len(metric_data))
            ax.bar(x, metric_data['forecast_value'], 
                   label='Forecast', color='steelblue', alpha=0.7)
            ax.bar(x, metric_data['actual_value'], 
                   label='Actual', color='navy', alpha=0.7, width=0.4)
            ax.set_xticks(x)
            ax.set_xticklabels(metric_data['period'], rotation=45, fontsize=8)
            ax.set_title(metric)
            ax.legend(fontsize=8)
            
            # Shade periods with >10% miss
            for i, row in enumerate(metric_data.itertuples()):
                if row.overoptimistic:
                    ax.axvspan(i - 0.5, i + 0.5, alpha=0.15, color='red')
        
        plt.tight_layout()
        return fig
```

## 11. Interview Questions

**Question 1:** "You are about to approve a £10m facility to a company with excellent financials. In the management meeting, the MD refuses to provide a list of the top 10 customers by name, saying it's commercially sensitive. What do you do?"

*Strong answer:* This is a transparency and integrity red flag, not a commercial sensitivity issue. A bank extending £10m of credit has a legitimate need to understand the revenue concentration of its borrower — that is standard credit due diligence, not a fishing expedition. I would explain this clearly to the MD. If the refusal persists, I would decline to proceed with the application. Information refusal at this stage tells you how the relationship will be in a stress scenario: if the MD won't share basic customer information before the money is lent, they certainly won't communicate proactively if the business is under pressure after the money is drawn. A credit relationship with an opaque borrower is a relationship where the bank has lent on false premises. Factor 4.1 and 4.2 in the management assessment scorecard would be scored at 9/10, which would flag the assessment as Very Weak and trigger escalation to the Senior Credit Officer with a recommendation to decline.

**Question 2:** "How do you assess management quality for a founder-led business where the founder is clearly the key-man?"

*Strong answer:* Key-man dependency is almost universal in founder-led mid-market businesses, so the question is not whether it exists but how severe it is and what mitigants are available. I assess severity across four dimensions: (1) revenue: are customer relationships personally owned by the founder or institutionalised? (2) knowledge: does the founder hold technical or operational knowledge that cannot be replicated? (3) finance: is there a capable CFO who could hold the business together if the founder departed suddenly? (4) legal: is the founder also the majority shareholder, creating a shareholder control risk as well as a management risk? Then I assess mitigants: is there key-man insurance in place with the bank noted as loss payee? Is there a written business continuity plan? Is there any second-tier management being developed? If key-man risk is severe, I reduce the maximum facility I would approve and require key-man insurance as a hard condition, not a recommendation.

**Question 3:** "What red flags in a company's audited accounts should prompt you to investigate management quality more deeply?"

*Strong answer:* Six primary red flags:
(1) **Qualified audit opinion or material uncertainty note** — especially relating to going concern, related-party transactions, or inventory/receivables valuation
(2) **Auditor change** — unexplained switch from a reputable firm to a smaller, less well-known practice
(3) **Large or growing related-party balances** — management fees to connected entities, director loans outstanding, property leases from connected parties at unexplained rates
(4) **Large gap between EBITDA and free cash flow (poor cash conversion)** — may indicate management accounts are being managed upward while cash exits through other channels
(5) **Goodwill impairment** — particularly if repeated, signals poor capital allocation judgement by management in acquisitions
(6) **Restatement of prior year comparatives** — indicates prior year accounts were misstated; relevant question is whether this was error or manipulation

**Question 4:** "A profitable borrower has just informed you that the Finance Director left suddenly two weeks ago. How do you respond?"

*Strong answer:* A sudden, unplanned senior management departure is a management event requiring immediate response. Steps: (1) call the relationship manager immediately to escalate — this is an early warning event that triggers a management event log entry; (2) request a meeting with the MD within 5 business days to understand the circumstances of the departure; (3) ask specifically: was the departure voluntary or involuntary? Are there any disputes, disagreements, or pending claims? Is the FD departing on good terms or not? (4) review the most recent management accounts for any anomalies that the FD might have been managing; (5) check that the FD departure will not breach any regulatory obligations the company has (e.g., if it is a regulated entity); (6) update the management quality scorecard — Factor 2.3 (financial management capability) and Factor 1.1 (management tenure) would both be reviewed; (7) consider whether an early financial review is warranted rather than waiting for the next scheduled annual review.

## 12. Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Confusing business success with management quality.**
A business that has been growing for five years in a buoyant market tells you relatively little about management quality. The easy mistake is to give management full credit for the business's success without understanding whether growth was driven by management skill or by favourable market tailwinds that lifted all boats. The diagnostic question is: has this management team performed in a downturn? Have they made hard decisions — redundancies, contract renegotiations, asset disposals — under pressure? A management team that has only operated in benign conditions has an unassessed resilience risk.

**Mistake 2: Over-relying on the management meeting impression.**
Management teams of businesses seeking bank finance are typically skilled at presenting their business positively in a management meeting. A charismatic, confident, articulate MD who knows their numbers can make a mediocre business seem very attractive in a two-hour meeting. The management meeting is necessary but not sufficient. It must be corroborated by the documentary evidence: the quality of management accounts, the accuracy of forecasts, the completeness of information provided, the absence of red flags in the Companies House filing. Trust but verify.

**Mistake 3: Failing to investigate related-party transactions adequately.**
Many analysts note related-party transactions in the audited accounts, confirm they are "disclosed" and move on. This is insufficient. Related-party transactions must be: (a) documented in detail (what is the transaction? who is the counterparty? what are the commercial terms?), (b) tested for arm's length (are the management fees, rents, or prices consistent with what an unconnected third party would pay?), and (c) assessed for materiality (a £15k management fee from a £20m turnover business is immaterial; a £400k management fee from a £3m EBITDA business is very material — it reduces cash available for debt service by 13%). RPTs that are not arm's length are potentially illegal under UK Companies Act 2006 and are a significant governance concern.

**Mistake 4: Not logging management events continuously.**
Management quality assessment is not an annual exercise. It is a continuous observation of management behaviour throughout the relationship. Banks that treat MQA as something done at annual review, and nothing more, miss the early warning signals that appear between reviews: the late management account submission, the covenant waiver that wasn't disclosed proactively, the FD who left quietly, the rumour in the trade press. The management event log must be maintained throughout the year by the Relationship Manager, and the credit team must review it at each credit assessment.

**Mistake 5: Not updating management assessments after a waiver or restructuring.**
When a bank grants a covenant waiver or agrees to a facility restructuring, it is because the borrower has underperformed against expectations. This is a material management event. Yet many banks grant the waiver and then move on without formally updating the management quality score. The waiver is evidence of forecast inaccuracy (Factor 4.3), potentially of reactive communication (Factor 4.2), and may indicate that the management team's judgement was flawed. The management quality score should be formally reviewed and updated following any covenant breach, waiver, or restructuring event.

## 13. Case Studies

### Case Study 1: PrecisionTech Engineering — Good Financials, Bad Management

**Background:** PrecisionTech Engineering Limited manufactures precision aerospace components. Annual revenue £9.2m (FY2023 forecast; actual £7.8m), EBITDA margin approximately 14% on actuals (£1.1m), Net Debt £3.4m, leverage 3.1x. The company applies to refinance £3.4m in term debt and increase its revolving credit facility from £500k to £1.5m to fund working capital for a new contract.

**On financials alone:** Leverage 3.1x EBITDA, DSCR approximately 1.8x — adequate, if tight. Industry (aerospace component manufacturing) is stable. Financial risk score would be approximately 4/10 (moderate). An analyst looking only at ratios might approve this.

**Business and management assessment findings:**

The management assessment process (scorecard above) produces a total MQA score of approximately 6.9/10 (WEAK). The specific findings:

*Key-man risk (score 8.5/10):* The MD, Julian Baker, is the sole point of contact for three customers representing 58% of revenue. He has no second-tier management below him in a commercial capacity. When the analyst asks what would happen if Julian were to become unavailable for three months due to illness, Julian says: "It would be difficult, but we'd manage." No specific plan is articulated. No key-man insurance exists. The FD, Paula Williams, has no relationship with any customer.

*Related-party transactions (score 8.0/10):* Three RPTs identified: (1) £180,000 per annum management fee to Baker Family Trust — no service agreement was produced, and when asked what services the trust provides, Julian says "business development and consulting," but no evidence of delivered services exists; (2) Julian's Range Rover on company lease at £12,000/year; (3) office premises rented from a property owned by Julian's wife at £42,000/year — the analyst checks Rightmove comparables and identifies similar premises in the area at £31,000–35,000/year, suggesting an above-market rental extracting approximately £7,000–11,000/year from the operating company to the director's personal asset. Total RPT value: approximately £234,000/year, against EBITDA of £1.1m — representing 21% of operating profit.

*Transparency (scores 7.0 and 7.5):* A review of the management account submission history shows that in 12 of the last 18 months, accounts were submitted more than 5 business days late. More significantly, a Q3-2023 leverage covenant breach went unreported for 6 weeks. Julian's explanation — "I thought it would self-correct" — represents a serious transparency failure: borrowers are required to notify the bank of covenant breaches promptly.

*Forecast accuracy:* FY2023 revenue forecast of £9.2m against actual of £7.8m is a 15% miss. FY2023 EBITDA forecast of £1.8m against actual of £1.1m is a 39% miss. At the meeting, Julian attributes this to "a delayed contract start" but when pressed cannot explain why the Q3 management accounts did not show any sign of this delay — suggesting the management accounts were not accurate during the year, or the variance was not communicated.

**Credit decision:**

The refinance of existing debt can be considered, but the RCF increase is declined at this time. Conditions imposed on the refinancing:

1. Key-man life and critical illness insurance (Julian Baker as life assured, bank as sole loss payee) minimum coverage = outstanding debt: £3.4m. Condition precedent to first drawdown.

2. All related-party transactions to be reviewed by the bank's legal advisers for arm's length pricing. Management fee services agreement to be provided. Office lease to be adjusted to market rate or evidence of arms-length valuation to be provided. If adjustments are made, covenant calculations to be recalibrated on the adjusted EBITDA.

3. Management accounts to be provided within 5 business days of each month-end (reduced from 10 business days). Failure to comply: technical event of default with 5-business-day cure period.

4. Covenant breach reporting obligation: any financial covenant breach to be notified to the bank within 3 business days of the company becoming aware.

5. Six-month review: formal management meeting at the 6-month mark, with specific review of management account quality and RPT management.

6. MQA score to be formally reviewed at 12 months. If score remains in WEAK band, the relationship to be referred to the Special Credits team for strategic review.

**Lesson:** This case illustrates how a competent credit analyst identifies and manages a credit where the financial position is manageable but the management risk is material. The credit is not declined — that would be overcautious given the viable financial position. Instead, the credit is structured with specific management-risk mitigants: insurance, governance conditions, enhanced reporting, and a structured review timeline. The management issues are documented explicitly in the credit paper so that the credit committee is making an informed decision.

### Case Study 2: Spotting the Integrity Red Flag Early

**Background:** A long-standing relationship customer. Family-owned business, engineering services, 12-year relationship with the bank. During the annual review, the analyst notices that the company changed auditors mid-year from a regional office of Grant Thornton to a sole-practitioner accountancy firm with no online presence. The annual accounts filed at Companies House show the change.

**The investigation:**

The analyst calls the MD to ask about the auditor change. The MD's explanation: "Grant Thornton quoted significantly higher fees. The new firm is perfectly competent." This is a plausible explanation, but the analyst notes: (1) Grant Thornton fees typically rise when an audit becomes more complex, time-consuming, or contentious; (2) a sole-practitioner audit firm has fewer resources to handle the accounts of a £15m-turnover business and has less regulatory oversight; (3) the timing of the change coincided with the audit of the period in which the company first exceeded its leverage covenant.

The analyst requests a copy of the previous auditor's resignation letter (under professional conduct standards, resigning auditors must provide a statement of circumstances). The company resists providing this. After escalation to the Senior Credit Officer, a formal information request is issued under the loan agreement's information covenants. The resignation letter, when eventually provided, states that the audit partner "was unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence in relation to certain intercompany transactions."

**Outcome:**

The "intercompany transactions" were discovered to be loans from the operating company (the bank's borrower) to a holding company entity also controlled by the family, totalling £2.3m. These loans were not disclosed on the management accounts provided to the bank, and the revolving credit facility was being used to fund cash flow shortfalls arising from cash being extracted to the holding company. This is a misuse of credit facilities and a potential fraud.

The bank moved immediately to demand formal confirmation of the company's financial position, appoint an investigating accountant, and place the facility on non-accrual. The relationship was subsequently moved to Special Credits for restructuring.

**Lesson:** The auditor change was the signal. An analyst who accepted the "higher fees" explanation without further inquiry would have missed a material integrity issue. The management event (auditor change) triggered an investigation. The investigation, pursued diligently despite management resistance, revealed the underlying problem while there was still asset value to recover against.

## 14. Iterative Reinforcement

**Week 1:**
1. Conduct a Companies House director search for a company you are currently assessing or studying. List all directors, their date of appointment, and any other current/historic directorships. Note any companies associated with those directors that have been dissolved or went into administration.
2. Read the auditor's report in a set of mid-market company accounts (any Companies House filing for a company with revenues > £10m). Identify: (a) is the opinion qualified or unqualified? (b) is there a going concern paragraph? (c) what key audit matters are identified?
3. Draft a management meeting agenda for a manufacturing company seeking a £5m term loan. Include 8–10 specific questions you would ask, and for each, explain what credit risk information you are trying to elicit.

**Week 2:**
1. Complete the Management Quality Scorecard for a real company. Use Companies House accounts, press searches, and (if available) management accounts from an existing relationship. Score every factor with written evidence.
2. Run the Python early warning detector on a simulated event dataset (generate 20 borrowers with random event histories). Review the alert ranking and identify the three highest-risk borrowers. For each, write a one-paragraph summary of the risk and proposed action.
3. Find an example of a management-driven corporate default in the financial press (Carillion, Thomas Cook, BHS, Ted Baker, Patisserie Valerie are well-documented UK examples). Read the post-mortem coverage. Identify which management quality factors from the scorecard were present as warning signs before the default. What could a credit analyst have identified earlier?

**Month 1 consolidation:**
Retrospectively assess the management quality of three borrowers in your portfolio where you have 2+ years of management account and meeting history. Score them using the MQA scorecard. Compare the management quality scores to the overall credit quality trajectory: have the lower-scored management teams performed worse financially? What is the time lag between management quality deterioration signals and financial deterioration? This exercise builds the empirical intuition that makes management assessment meaningful.
