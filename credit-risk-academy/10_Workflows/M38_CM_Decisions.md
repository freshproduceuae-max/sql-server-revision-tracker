# M38 — Credit Manager Decision-Making

## 1. Business Purpose

The Credit Manager (CM) sits at the apex of the first-line credit decision process. Their role is to evaluate credit papers submitted by Credit Analysts, ask the right challenge questions, and make credit decisions — approvals, conditional approvals, or declines — within their delegated authority limits.

The CM's dual mandate is often described as: approve good business and decline bad risk. Both halves of this mandate carry equal weight. A CM who is overly conservative declines transactions that would have been profitable and drives business to competitors; a CM who is insufficiently rigorous approves transactions that generate losses. The CM is measured on both dimensions — portfolio loss experience and new business volumes — though the credit cycle means losses are often only visible years after decisions are made.

In UK corporate banking, CMs typically hold delegated authority to approve facilities up to a defined size (e.g., £10m for a Grade 5 CM, £25m for a Grade 6, £50m for a Head of Credit) within policy and subject to minimum credit quality standards. Larger facilities, policy exceptions, or borderline credits are escalated to a credit committee or higher authority.

The CM builds a portfolio view over time. Individual credit decisions must be assessed not only on their standalone merits but in the context of the existing portfolio — sector concentration, obligor concentration, rating distribution, and overall risk appetite consumption. A credit that would be acceptable in isolation may not be acceptable if the bank already has 15% of its corporate portfolio in that sector.

The CM also acts as a coach to the credit analyst and as a check on the RM. If a credit paper is of poor quality, the CM returns it for rework rather than making a decision on incomplete information. If the RM is advocating for a transaction that does not meet credit standards, the CM declines or conditions the approval to bring it into policy.

## 2. Accounting Concepts

**Reading Financials at the Decision Level:** The CM does not re-spread the financials — that is the CA's job. The CM reads the credit paper's financial summary and ratio table, then dives deeper into specific items that create doubt or require explanation. The CM asks: do these numbers make sense? Is the trend consistent? Are the key ratios plausible for this industry?

**Accounting Red Flags:** A seasoned CM recognises warning signs in financial presentations:
- Significant gap between EBITDA and operating cash flow suggests aggressive revenue recognition or working capital deterioration.
- Rising goodwill without explanation suggests serial acquisitions at elevated prices.
- Rapidly growing "other debtors" on the balance sheet may indicate related party receivables or deferred revenue being capitalised.
- A consistent pattern of exceptional items is evidence that the items are not exceptional.
- Revenue that grows without a corresponding increase in debtors (in a credit-based business) warrants explanation.

**Management vs Audited Accounts:** The CM understands that management accounts are unaudited. They may be prepared to present the business in the best light. The CM asks: has the CA explained material differences between the management accounts and the prior year audited accounts? Are the management accounts consistently prepared, or have definitions changed?

**Going Concern:** If the auditor has expressed any doubt about going concern, the CM requires a full explanation before considering approval. Lending to a business with a qualified going concern opinion without understanding the specific concerns and mitigants is an unacceptable credit risk practice.

## 3. Financial Concepts

**The Five Cs of Credit:** The CM's evaluation framework, applied to every credit decision:

1. **Character:** The willingness of the borrower to repay. Assessment draws on: management track record, credit history (any prior defaults or restructurings?), quality of the business plan, transparency in dealings with the bank, and governance quality (board independence, auditor quality). Character cannot be measured quantitatively but is the foundation of all credit decisions — a borrower with strong financials but poor character is still a risk.

2. **Capacity:** The ability of the borrower to generate sufficient cash flow to service the debt. Measured by DSCR, interest cover, and FCF. The CM assesses not just current capacity but projected capacity under stress. The primary repayment source is always cash flow from operations; secondary sources (asset sales, refinancing) are not relied upon for standard bilateral facilities.

3. **Capital:** The borrower's own financial resources — equity, retained earnings, net worth. The CM asks: does the borrower have skin in the game? Is there meaningful equity supporting the loan, or is the structure highly leveraged with minimal equity cushion? For property loans, loan-to-value (LTV) is the capital measure. For corporate loans, tangible net worth relative to debt provides the capital perspective.

4. **Collateral:** The security taken by the bank to protect recovery if the borrower defaults. The CM evaluates: what security is being taken, has it been valued by an appropriate independent party, is the valuation current, and what is the realistic recovery value in a distressed sale? Collateral is the secondary repayment source, not the primary. A CM who approves a credit solely on the strength of collateral while ignoring cash flow adequacy is making a structural mistake.

5. **Conditions:** The economic and industry environment in which the borrower operates, plus the specific conditions imposed on the credit. Macro conditions (interest rate cycle, economic growth outlook, sector-specific trends) affect the CM's confidence in projections. Credit conditions are the covenants, reporting requirements, and structural features that the CM imposes to manage risk on an ongoing basis.

**Reading a Credit Paper Efficiently:** A CM reviewing 5–10 papers per week must extract key information quickly. The sequence: (1) Executive summary — what is the borrower, what is the request, what is the recommendation? (2) Risk rating and IFRS 9 stage — is this a borderline case? (3) Key financials table — DSCR, leverage, TNW at a glance. (4) Structure section — is the facility appropriate for the purpose? (5) Security section — what collateral backs this credit? (6) Covenant section — is there adequate protection? (7) Policy exceptions — any departures from standard policy? (8) Full paper — areas of concern identified from the executive summary scan.

## 4. Statistical Concepts

**Expected Loss and the Credit Decision:** The CM understands that every credit approval generates an expected loss (EL = PD × LGD × EAD). The CM asks: is the margin earned on this facility sufficient to compensate for the expected loss and still generate an adequate return on capital? A facility pricing at SONIA + 1.50% on a borrower with a 3% PD and 50% LGD is not profitable after EL is deducted.

**Tail Risk:** Beyond EL, the CM considers unexpected loss — the loss in a severe downside scenario. The capital held against a facility is sized to cover unexpected loss at a high confidence level (99.9% in Basel III). The CM's portfolio management responsibility includes ensuring that the portfolio's unexpected loss profile is within the bank's risk appetite.

**Correlation and Concentration Risk:** The CM monitors sector and geographic concentration. When a CM's portfolio has 30% of drawn exposure in a single sector, adding more credits in that sector increases correlated loss risk — the chance that multiple borrowers default simultaneously due to a shared macro shock. The CM may decline an otherwise acceptable credit if it would breach sector concentration limits.

**Vintage Analysis:** Credit decisions made in different economic environments have different default rates. A CM should be aware of vintage effects — credits originated at the peak of an economic cycle, when pricing was tight and underwriting standards relaxed, perform worse than credits originated in a tighter underwriting environment. The CM adjusts their standards over the cycle, tightening during periods of loose credit conditions.

## 5. Regulatory Framework

**Delegated Authority Framework:** UK banks maintain a credit approval authority framework that specifies: maximum exposure a given approval level can sanction, minimum credit quality required for single-signature approval (bilateral limits), requirements for second signature, and types of decision requiring credit committee approval. The CM operates within this framework and must escalate any credit that exceeds their authority.

**Three Lines of Defence:** The CM in the first line (1LoD) approves credits within policy. The second line (2LoD) — credit risk management — reviews the portfolio, monitors credit quality, challenges underwriting standards, and reports to the Board Risk Committee. The third line (3LoD) — internal audit — periodically reviews whether the credit approval process is working as designed. The CM cooperates with 2LoD review without viewing it as adversarial.

**PRA/FCA Expectations:** The PRA expects CM decisions to be documented with sufficient rationale that an independent reviewer could understand the basis for the decision. The PRA's supervisory statement on IRB approaches (SS11/13) sets expectations around rating assignment, override documentation, and portfolio quality monitoring.

**Material Risk Takers:** CMs above a certain seniority and compensation threshold are classified as Material Risk Takers (MRTs) under CRD IV / CRD V. This subjects their variable compensation to deferral, clawback, and malus provisions. The regulatory intention is that CMs should bear personal financial consequences for credit decisions that later prove to have been poorly made.

**Conflicts of Interest:** A CM must not approve a credit where they have a personal financial interest in the outcome. Connected lending (lending to related parties) requires escalation regardless of the CM's authority level. The CM's credit decisions should be made on the merits, free from commercial pressure, and the CM must be willing to decline business from the bank's most important clients if the credit risk is unacceptable.

## 6. Data Required

The CM needs the following information at the point of decision:

- **The credit paper** in final draft form, signed off by the Credit Analyst and reviewed for completeness.
- **Group exposure summary:** All current and proposed exposures to the obligor and connected parties.
- **Portfolio concentration report:** Current sector and geographic concentration of the portfolio against limits.
- **Risk rating history:** Has the borrower's rating been stable, improving, or deteriorating?
- **Covenant compliance history:** Has the borrower been fully compliant, or have waivers been required?
- **Account conduct history:** Any excesses, late payments, or returned items?
- **Policy exceptions list:** Any departures from standard credit policy that require the CM to confirm they understand the increased risk.
- **Comparable credits:** For pricing and structuring decisions, comparable facilities recently approved in the same sector provide a benchmark.

## 7. How Analysts Actually Work

**Receiving the Credit Paper:** The CM receives the credit paper via the workflow system. Before reading it substantively, the CM checks: is it complete, has the CA signed it off, are all required attachments present? An incomplete submission is returned immediately.

**Reading the Executive Summary:** The CM reads the executive summary first. A well-written executive summary enables the CM to identify the key risks and the key question within five minutes. If the executive summary is unclear or hedged, the CM may return the paper for rewriting before investing time in the detail.

**Challenge Questions:** The CM generates challenge questions in reading order. These are the questions that a rigorous, independent examiner would ask about this credit. Common challenge questions:
- What happens to DSCR if revenue falls 15%?
- Why is the debt increasing while EBITDA is growing?
- How was the security valuation obtained and is it current?
- What is the basis for the management's revenue growth assumption?
- Has the CA challenged the management accounts, or are they accepted uncritically?
- What is the exit strategy if this business underperforms?
- Are there any cross-default provisions in the existing debt that would be triggered by this new facility?

**The Approval Conversation:** The CM discusses challenge questions with the CA (and sometimes the RM) either in person or via a written query process. The CM does not accept "the RM told me" as an answer — the CA should have independently verified all material facts.

**Writing the Approval Decision:** Every credit approval must be documented with a clear rationale. The approval note must include: summary of the request, key credit strengths, key credit risks, conditions attached to the approval (if any), and the basis for the CM's decision. A one-line approval ("approved as presented") is not adequate for any credit above the simplest SME overdraft.

**Conditions and Covenants as Risk Management Tools:** The CM uses conditions to manage residual risk. Conditions precedent to drawdown include: satisfactory security being in place, CPs being met, no material adverse change since approval. Ongoing conditions include financial covenants (DSCR, leverage, interest cover), reporting requirements (annual accounts within a specified period, management accounts quarterly, covenant compliance certificate), and restrictions on dividends, acquisitions, and additional indebtedness.

**Declining Credits:** A decline is as important as an approval. The CM writes a decline rationale that: explains specifically why the credit is not supportable, identifies what changes (if any) would make the credit approvable, and avoids language that could be interpreted as a guarantee of future approval. A good decline note protects the bank legally and helps the RM understand why the business cannot be supported.

**Building the Portfolio View:** The CM maintains a mental model (supported by data) of their portfolio's risk distribution. They know: which credits are the largest exposures, which sectors are most heavily represented, which credits are most susceptible to an interest rate increase, and which borrowers are approaching covenant triggers. This portfolio view informs how they calibrate individual credit decisions.

## 9. SQL Implementation

```sql
-- ===== CREDIT DECISION LOG QUERY =====
SELECT
    cd.decision_date,
    c.client_name,
    c.sector,
    f.facility_amount,
    f.facility_type,
    c.internal_rating,
    cd.decision,           -- 'APPROVED', 'DECLINED', 'CONDITIONAL', 'REFERRED_UP'
    cd.approver_name,
    cd.has_policy_exception,
    cd.approval_conditions,
    cd.rationale_summary
FROM credit_decisions cd
JOIN clients c ON cd.client_id = c.client_id
JOIN facilities f ON cd.facility_id = f.facility_id
WHERE cd.decision_date >= DATEADD(MONTH, -3, GETDATE())
ORDER BY cd.decision_date DESC;

-- ===== APPROVAL RATE BY RISK RATING =====
SELECT
    c.internal_rating,
    COUNT(*) AS total_decisions,
    SUM(CASE WHEN cd.decision = 'APPROVED' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS approved,
    SUM(CASE WHEN cd.decision = 'DECLINED' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS declined,
    ROUND(SUM(CASE WHEN cd.decision = 'APPROVED' THEN 1.0 ELSE 0 END)
          / COUNT(*) * 100, 1) AS approval_rate_pct
FROM credit_decisions cd
JOIN clients c ON cd.client_id = c.client_id
WHERE cd.decision_date >= DATEADD(YEAR, -1, GETDATE())
GROUP BY c.internal_rating
ORDER BY c.internal_rating;

-- ===== PORTFOLIO SECTOR CONCENTRATION =====
SELECT
    c.sector,
    COUNT(DISTINCT c.client_id) AS client_count,
    SUM(f.approved_limit) AS total_limit_m,
    SUM(f.current_drawn) AS total_drawn_m,
    SUM(f.approved_limit) / SUM(SUM(f.approved_limit)) OVER () * 100
        AS pct_of_total_limit,
    sl.sector_limit_pct,
    SUM(f.approved_limit) / SUM(SUM(f.approved_limit)) OVER () * 100
        - sl.sector_limit_pct AS headroom_pct
FROM clients c
JOIN facilities f ON c.client_id = f.client_id
JOIN sector_limits sl ON c.sector = sl.sector
WHERE f.status = 'ACTIVE'
GROUP BY c.sector, sl.sector_limit_pct
ORDER BY pct_of_total_limit DESC;

-- ===== CONDITIONS TRACKING =====
-- Monitor whether conditions attached to approvals have been satisfied
SELECT
    c.client_name,
    cd.decision_date,
    cc.condition_description,
    cc.condition_type,         -- 'CP', 'ONGOING', 'COVENANT'
    cc.due_date,
    cc.satisfied_date,
    CASE
        WHEN cc.satisfied_date IS NOT NULL THEN 'SATISFIED'
        WHEN cc.due_date < GETDATE() THEN 'OVERDUE'
        ELSE 'PENDING'
    END AS condition_status
FROM credit_conditions cc
JOIN credit_decisions cd ON cc.decision_id = cd.decision_id
JOIN clients c ON cd.client_id = c.client_id
WHERE cc.satisfied_date IS NULL
ORDER BY cc.due_date ASC;

-- ===== CM DECISION QUALITY RETROSPECTIVE =====
-- Assess decisions that subsequently defaulted
SELECT
    cd.approver_name,
    cd.decision_date,
    c.client_name,
    c.internal_rating AS rating_at_approval,
    f.facility_amount,
    cd.decision,
    dd.default_date,
    dd.loss_amount,
    DATEDIFF(MONTH, cd.decision_date, dd.default_date) AS months_to_default
FROM credit_decisions cd
JOIN clients c ON cd.client_id = c.client_id
JOIN facilities f ON cd.facility_id = f.facility_id
JOIN defaults dd ON c.client_id = dd.client_id
WHERE cd.decision = 'APPROVED'
ORDER BY cd.decision_date;
```

## 10. Python Implementation

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import List, Optional, Tuple
from enum import Enum


class Decision(Enum):
    APPROVED = "APPROVED"
    CONDITIONAL = "CONDITIONAL"
    DECLINED = "DECLINED"
    REFERRED_UP = "REFERRED_UP"


# ===== FIVE Cs CREDIT SCORING ENGINE =====

@dataclass
class FiveCsAssessment:
    """Structured credit assessment using the Five Cs framework."""

    # Character (1=Poor, 5=Excellent)
    management_track_record: int
    credit_history: int         # 1=prior default, 5=clean
    transparency_with_bank: int
    corporate_governance: int

    # Capacity (financial ratios, actuals)
    dscr: float
    interest_cover: float
    leverage_x: float
    fcf_m: float

    # Capital
    tnw_m: float
    net_debt_m: float
    equity_to_assets_pct: float

    # Collateral
    ltv_pct: Optional[float]       # None if unsecured
    security_description: str

    # Conditions
    industry_outlook: int          # 1=very negative, 5=very positive
    macro_environment: int         # 1=recession imminent, 5=benign
    facility_purpose_quality: int  # 1=vague, 5=clear and appropriate

    def character_score(self) -> float:
        return np.mean([
            self.management_track_record,
            self.credit_history,
            self.transparency_with_bank,
            self.corporate_governance
        ])

    def capacity_score(self) -> float:
        dscr_score = (5 if self.dscr >= 2.0
                      else 4 if self.dscr >= 1.5
                      else 3 if self.dscr >= 1.25
                      else 2 if self.dscr >= 1.0
                      else 1)
        ic_score = (5 if self.interest_cover >= 5.0
                    else 4 if self.interest_cover >= 3.0
                    else 3 if self.interest_cover >= 2.0
                    else 2 if self.interest_cover >= 1.5
                    else 1)
        lev_score = (5 if self.leverage_x <= 1.5
                     else 4 if self.leverage_x <= 2.5
                     else 3 if self.leverage_x <= 3.5
                     else 2 if self.leverage_x <= 4.5
                     else 1)
        return np.mean([dscr_score, ic_score, lev_score])

    def capital_score(self) -> float:
        tnw_to_debt = self.tnw_m / self.net_debt_m if self.net_debt_m > 0 else 5.0
        tnw_score = (5 if tnw_to_debt >= 1.0
                     else 4 if tnw_to_debt >= 0.5
                     else 3 if tnw_to_debt >= 0.25
                     else 2 if tnw_to_debt >= 0.1
                     else 1)
        eq_score = (5 if self.equity_to_assets_pct >= 40
                    else 4 if self.equity_to_assets_pct >= 25
                    else 3 if self.equity_to_assets_pct >= 15
                    else 2 if self.equity_to_assets_pct >= 5
                    else 1)
        return np.mean([tnw_score, eq_score])

    def collateral_score(self) -> float:
        if self.ltv_pct is None:
            return 2.0  # unsecured gets a lower collateral score
        return (5 if self.ltv_pct <= 50
                else 4 if self.ltv_pct <= 65
                else 3 if self.ltv_pct <= 75
                else 2 if self.ltv_pct <= 85
                else 1)

    def conditions_score(self) -> float:
        return np.mean([
            self.industry_outlook,
            self.macro_environment,
            self.facility_purpose_quality
        ])

    def overall_score(self,
                      weights=(0.25, 0.35, 0.15, 0.10, 0.15)) -> float:
        """Weighted average of all five C scores. Weights: C, Cap, Cap, Col, Con."""
        scores = [
            self.character_score(),
            self.capacity_score(),
            self.capital_score(),
            self.collateral_score(),
            self.conditions_score()
        ]
        return sum(w * s for w, s in zip(weights, scores))

    def suggested_rating(self) -> str:
        score = self.overall_score()
        if score >= 4.5: return "1-2 (Investment Grade)"
        if score >= 3.5: return "3-4 (Sub-Investment Grade, Low Risk)"
        if score >= 2.5: return "5-6 (Speculative)"
        if score >= 1.5: return "7-8 (Watch)"
        return "9-10 (Distressed)"

    def full_report(self) -> dict:
        return {
            'Character Score': round(self.character_score(), 2),
            'Capacity Score': round(self.capacity_score(), 2),
            'Capital Score': round(self.capital_score(), 2),
            'Collateral Score': round(self.collateral_score(), 2),
            'Conditions Score': round(self.conditions_score(), 2),
            'Overall Score': round(self.overall_score(), 2),
            'Suggested Rating': self.suggested_rating()
        }


# ===== CREDIT DECISION ENGINE =====

@dataclass
class CreditRequest:
    client_name: str
    facility_amount_m: float
    facility_type: str
    purpose: str
    dscr: float
    leverage_x: float
    internal_rating: int         # 1-10
    ltv_pct: Optional[float]
    has_policy_exception: bool
    policy_exception_detail: str = ""

POLICY_RULES = {
    'max_leverage': 4.5,
    'min_dscr': 1.20,
    'max_ltv': 80.0,
    'max_rating_unsecured': 7,
    'max_rating_secured': 8
}

def credit_decision_engine(request: CreditRequest) -> Tuple[Decision, List[str]]:
    """
    Rule-based credit decision engine applying credit policy.
    Returns decision and list of issues/conditions.
    """
    issues = []
    hard_declines = []

    # Hard policy limits
    if request.leverage_x > POLICY_RULES['max_leverage']:
        hard_declines.append(
            f"Leverage {request.leverage_x:.1f}x exceeds policy max "
            f"{POLICY_RULES['max_leverage']}x"
        )
    if request.dscr < POLICY_RULES['min_dscr']:
        hard_declines.append(
            f"DSCR {request.dscr:.2f}x below policy minimum "
            f"{POLICY_RULES['min_dscr']}x"
        )
    if request.ltv_pct and request.ltv_pct > POLICY_RULES['max_ltv']:
        hard_declines.append(
            f"LTV {request.ltv_pct:.1f}% exceeds policy max "
            f"{POLICY_RULES['max_ltv']}%"
        )

    # Hard decline on hard policy violations (without exception approval)
    if hard_declines and not request.has_policy_exception:
        return Decision.DECLINED, hard_declines

    # Conditions for marginal credits
    if request.dscr < 1.35:
        issues.append("CONDITION: Quarterly management accounts required given thin DSCR headroom")
    if request.leverage_x > 3.5:
        issues.append("CONDITION: Annual independent valuation of security required")
    if request.internal_rating >= 7:
        issues.append("CONDITION: Place on watch list; monthly monitoring required")
    if request.has_policy_exception:
        issues.append(f"NOTE: Policy exception approved — {request.policy_exception_detail}")

    # Referral up for larger amounts or weaker credits
    if request.facility_amount_m > 25.0 or request.internal_rating >= 7:
        return Decision.REFERRED_UP, issues + ["Referral required: amount/rating exceeds CM authority"]

    if hard_declines:  # With approved exception
        return Decision.CONDITIONAL, issues + hard_declines
    if issues:
        return Decision.CONDITIONAL, issues
    return Decision.APPROVED, ["Approved within policy. Standard conditions apply."]


# ===== PORTFOLIO CONCENTRATION CHECK =====

def concentration_check(portfolio_df: pd.DataFrame,
                         sector_limits: dict,
                         new_sector: str,
                         new_amount_m: float) -> dict:
    """Check whether adding a new credit breaches sector concentration limits."""
    sector_totals = portfolio_df.groupby('sector')['approved_limit'].sum()
    total_portfolio = portfolio_df['approved_limit'].sum() + new_amount_m

    current_pct = sector_totals.get(new_sector, 0) / total_portfolio * 100
    new_pct = (sector_totals.get(new_sector, 0) + new_amount_m) / total_portfolio * 100
    limit_pct = sector_limits.get(new_sector, 20.0)  # default 20%

    return {
        'sector': new_sector,
        'current_pct': round(current_pct, 1),
        'post_addition_pct': round(new_pct, 1),
        'limit_pct': limit_pct,
        'headroom_pct': round(limit_pct - new_pct, 1),
        'breach': new_pct > limit_pct
    }
```

## 11. Interview Questions

**Q1: How do you distinguish between a credit that is weak but approvable versus a credit that should be declined?**
A: A weak-but-approvable credit has identifiable risks that can be managed through structure, covenants, pricing, and security. The CM can see a realistic base case where the debt is repaid from operations, even if the margin for error is limited. A decline is appropriate when: the primary repayment source is genuinely uncertain, the security is insufficient and unreliable, character concerns make the CM doubt management's intentions, or the credit violates fundamental policy parameters without adequate mitigation.

**Q2: A senior RM comes to you with a credit for a long-standing client, insisting it must be approved by end of the week. How do you handle the pressure?**
A: Commercial pressure from an RM is not a legitimate basis for a credit decision. I make the decision on the merits, on the timeline required to do it properly. If the RM's timeline is unrealistic, I explain the analysis required and give a realistic completion date. I also note that rushing a credit decision is itself a risk indicator — legitimate business needs are rarely so time-critical that proper analysis is impossible.

**Q3: What conditions would you attach to the approval of a leveraged acquisition?**
A: Financial covenants (DSCR, leverage, interest cover), tested quarterly. An equity cure mechanism (limited to twice in any 12 months). Restrictions on additional indebtedness, disposals, and dividends. Information undertakings including audited accounts within 180 days, quarterly management accounts, and prompt notification of material events. A clean-down clause for revolving facilities (ensures the RCF is genuinely used for working capital, not as permanent leverage). Security over key assets of the acquirer and target.

**Q4: How do you build a portfolio view, and why does it matter for individual credit decisions?**
A: I track my portfolio by sector concentration, rating distribution, average DSCR and leverage, and exposure to interest rate sensitivity (floating vs fixed rate borrowers). This matters because individual credits do not exist in isolation. Adding a credit that is individually acceptable but pushes my retail sector concentration from 25% to 35% is a portfolio risk I must recognise. In a retail downturn, my portfolio would suffer correlated defaults.

**Q5: Describe a situation where you would decline a credit despite strong financial metrics.**
A: Strong financials do not override character concerns. If I have evidence that management has been misleading — presenting manipulated management accounts, concealing material liabilities, or failing to disclose related party transactions — I decline regardless of the financial metrics. The numbers a dishonest management presents cannot be relied upon. Similarly, if the purpose of the facility is unclear or the structure suggests that funds will flow to undisclosed purposes, I decline.

## 12. Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Approving a credit because the security covers the exposure.** Security is the secondary repayment source, not the primary. Approving a credit purely on security adequacy without assessing cash flow is a structural error. In a default, security realisation involves cost, delay, and often shortfall.

**Mistake 2: Deferring to the RM's assessment of management quality.** The CM cannot meet every borrower. But the CM should ask the CA: what specific evidence underpins the management quality assessment? Track record (number of years, performance), governance structure, management information quality, and external references should support the qualitative assessment.

**Mistake 3: Writing approval notes with insufficient rationale.** "Approved as presented" is inadequate. An audit, a 2LoD review, or a court in a later dispute will want to understand why the CM approved the credit. The rationale must address the specific risks and explain why they are acceptable.

**Mistake 4: Failing to track conditions after approval.** Conditions are only effective if they are enforced. The CM must ensure that the operations team tracks CP satisfaction, and that the RM reports back on ongoing conditions. A condition that is never monitored provides no risk protection.

**Mistake 5: Inconsistent application of credit standards.** Approving credits at 4.5x leverage for some borrowers and declining at 3.5x for others without documented rationale creates legal and regulatory risk. Credit standards must be applied consistently and documented.

## 13. Case Studies

**Case Study 1: The Relationship Override**
A CM is presented with a credit for a key client — a family-owned property group that has banked with the institution for 20 years and provides significant FX and treasury income. The credit analyst's paper shows leverage at 5.2x and DSCR of 1.05x, both outside policy. The RM argues the relationship is too important to lose. The CM declines the credit on the current structure, counter-proposes a structure with a 30% equity injection (reducing leverage to 3.6x) and phased drawdown tied to rental income milestones. The client initially threatens to move but ultimately accepts the revised structure. Two years later, the property market softens; the equity injection and phased structure protect the bank.

**Case Study 2: The Character Red Flag**
A manufacturing borrower requests a £12m acquisition finance facility. Financials are strong (DSCR 1.7x, leverage 2.8x). During the CM's review, they note that the CA's paper mentions management accounts "provided by the client" without auditor review. The CM asks whether the CA has reconciled the management accounts to the most recent audited accounts. The CA has not. On reconciliation, the CA discovers that EBITDA in the management accounts is £600k higher than the audited equivalent period, due to revenue recognised on a contract not yet completed. The CM requests an explanation from management, who initially resist. The CM declines the credit, citing inability to rely on management information.

## 14. Iterative Reinforcement

**Week 1 — Five Cs Practice:** Take a credit paper (real or anonymised) and score each of the Five Cs from 1 to 5 with documented rationale. Compare your scores to a senior colleague's assessment.

**Week 2 — Challenge Questions:** Read five executive summaries and generate ten challenge questions for each, without reading the full paper first. Then read the full paper and assess whether your questions were answered.

**Week 3 — Approval Note Drafting:** Write a one-page approval note for a straightforward credit, including all key conditions and rationale. Have a senior CM review it.

**Week 4 — Portfolio View:** Pull the current portfolio concentration report for your book. Identify the three highest concentration sectors and assess what a 20% revenue decline in each sector would do to your portfolio loss rate.

## 15. Source Material

- **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, "Principles for the Management of Credit Risk" (2000):** The foundational global framework for credit risk management in banks — defines the CM's role and responsibilities.
- **PRA Supervisory Statement SS11/13:** "Internal Ratings Based (IRB) approaches" — UK regulatory expectations for credit rating processes and override documentation.
- **UK Finance / BBA "Commercial Lending Code":** Market standards for commercial credit practices in the UK.
- **LMA Credit Agreement User Guide:** Practical guide to commercial loan documentation relevant to structuring conditions and covenants.
- **"The Five Cs of Credit" — American Bankers Association:** Classic framework taught in commercial banking training programmes globally.
- **FDIC (US) "Loan Portfolio Management" handbook:** Though US-focused, the portfolio management principles are internationally applicable and widely used in UK banking training.
- **"Bank Management and Financial Services" by Peter Rose and Sylvia Hudgins (McGraw-Hill):** Textbook covering credit decision-making, delegated authority, and portfolio management.
- **Moody's Analytics Credit Training:** "Fundamentals of Credit Analysis" — widely used in UK and European commercial banking training programmes.
- **CFA Institute, "Fixed Income Analysis":** Chapter on credit analysis provides the investment-grade perspective applicable to CM decisions on larger corporate credits.
- **"Credit Culture: How to Build a Healthy Credit Risk Environment":** Industry discussion paper from the Risk Management Association (RMA) on building sound credit decision-making cultures.
