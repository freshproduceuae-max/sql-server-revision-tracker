# M37 — Credit Analyst Workflow

## 1. Business Purpose

The Credit Analyst (CA) is the engine of the credit underwriting process. In commercial and corporate banking, the CA receives credit requests from Relationship Managers, performs independent financial and risk analysis, and produces the written credit memorandum (credit paper) that forms the basis for a credit decision.

The CA's fundamental purpose is to provide an objective, evidence-based assessment of a borrower's creditworthiness — independent of the RM's commercial interest in winning or retaining the business. This independence is not merely procedural; it is a core safeguard against relationship-driven lending, where the desire to maintain client goodwill overrides sound credit judgment.

The CA does not approve or decline credits — that is the Credit Manager's role. The CA's job is to ensure that the Credit Manager has a complete, accurate, and balanced picture of the credit risk before making a decision. A well-written credit paper presents the full picture: the strengths that support the request and the risks that must be weighed against approval.

In well-structured banks, the CA reports to the Credit Manager or Head of Credit, not to the RM. This organisational separation reinforces analytical independence. In smaller institutions, the CA may report to the Head of Commercial Banking, but the principle of independence must still be maintained.

Senior Credit Analysts and Credit Associates (the more junior tier) handle different complexity levels. A Credit Associate might process straightforward annual reviews for sub-investment-grade SMEs; a Senior Credit Analyst handles complex new money transactions, leveraged finance packages, or multi-facility syndicated deals. Over time, analysts develop sector expertise that makes their analysis more nuanced and their challenge questions more effective.

## 2. Accounting Concepts

The CA must master financial statement spreading — the systematic process of extracting, normalising, and presenting financial data from borrower accounts in a standard format that enables trend analysis and ratio calculation.

**Spreading Financial Statements:** The CA extracts data from audited accounts (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement, notes to accounts) into a spreading template. Key normalisation adjustments include: removing one-off or exceptional items from EBITDA (restructuring charges, asset disposal gains/losses), capitalising operating leases under pre-IFRS 16 standards for comparability, eliminating intercompany transactions in entity-level analysis, and adjusting for pension deficit contributions that are cash outflows but not P&L charges.

**Audit Opinions:** Before spreading any accounts, the CA reads the auditor's report. A qualified audit opinion, an emphasis of matter regarding going concern, or a disclaimer of opinion are significant red flags requiring immediate escalation and explanation in the credit paper. Accounts prepared on a basis other than going concern should generally not support a new credit request.

**Accounting Policy Changes:** If a borrower changes accounting policies between years (e.g., changes revenue recognition, changes depreciation methods, or first adopts IFRS 16 for leases), the CA must restate comparatives to assess the underlying trend. Apparent improvements in financials resulting from policy changes, not underlying performance, must be identified and disclosed.

**Normalised EBITDA:** The CA calculates normalised EBITDA by starting with operating profit, adding back depreciation and amortisation, then adjusting for: exceptional items (one-off restructuring, litigation settlements), management bonuses that will not recur, and the owner's salary in an owner-managed business (if above or below market rate). The adjusted figure is used for leverage and coverage ratio analysis.

**Revenue Recognition:** The CA must understand how the borrower recognises revenue, particularly for construction companies (percentage of completion vs contract completion), software companies (licence vs SaaS), and property developers (sale vs development completion). Aggressive revenue recognition inflates reported profits without cash generation.

## 3. Financial Concepts

**Financial Statement Spreading:** A three-to-five year spread provides trend context. Single-year analysis is insufficient. The CA looks for: revenue growth trajectory (organic vs acquired), margin trends (expanding or contracting EBITDA margin), working capital movements (cash conversion cycle lengthening or shortening), capex patterns (maintenance vs growth), and debt trajectory (net debt rising or falling relative to EBITDA).

**Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR):** DSCR = (EBITDA − Tax − Capex − Working Capital Change) / (Interest + Principal Repayments). The CA calculates DSCR using actual cash flow, not proxy EBITDA. A DSCR below 1.0x means the business cannot service its debt from operations alone. Most banks require DSCR of at least 1.25x for secured lending, 1.5x for unsecured.

**Leverage:** Total net debt / EBITDA. The leverage ratio is the primary sizing metric for corporate loans. A borrower at 4.0x leverage is meaningfully more risky than one at 1.5x leverage, all else equal. The CA must also test leverage under stress — if EBITDA falls 20%, does leverage remain within covenant headroom?

**Interest Cover:** EBITDA / Net Interest Expense. Typically required to be at least 2.0x to 3.0x. A declining interest cover ratio, even if still above covenant minimum, is a trend worth highlighting in the credit paper.

**Free Cash Flow (FCF):** FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capex. The CA must distinguish between maintenance capex (keeping the business operating) and growth capex (expanding capacity). Maintenance capex is unavoidable; stripping it out flatters FCF. The CA calculates FCF on both a maintenance capex basis and a total capex basis.

**Tangible Net Worth (TNW):** Total equity minus intangible assets (goodwill, customer relationships, licences). TNW represents the hard asset backing of the business. A borrower with £50m reported equity but £45m of goodwill has only £5m TNW — a thin cushion against losses.

**Net Debt Reconciliation:** The CA reconciles net debt from the balance sheet (total borrowings minus cash) and traces changes year-over-year. An increase in net debt despite positive EBITDA suggests cash leakage via: capex, working capital build, tax payments, dividends, or acquisitions. Each element should be explained.

## 4. Statistical Concepts

**Sensitivity Analysis:** The CA builds a base case financial model and then stress-tests it. Sensitivities typically include: revenue decline of 10%, 20%, and 30%; margin compression of 2 percentage points; interest rate increase of 1% and 2% (relevant for floating rate borrowers); and a combination scenario (revenue down 15%, margin down 1pp). For each scenario, the CA recalculates DSCR, leverage, and TNW, and assesses whether covenants would be breached.

**Internal Credit Rating:** The CA assigns or proposes an internal credit rating based on the bank's rating methodology. Rating factors typically include: financial performance (weighted ~40%), industry risk (~20%), management quality (~15%), competitive position (~15%), and country/macro risk (~10%). The CA documents the rating rationale explicitly, referencing specific financial metrics and qualitative factors.

**PD Calibration:** The assigned rating maps to a probability of default (PD). The CA should understand the mapping: for example, an internal rating of 5 might correspond to a PD of 0.5% per annum, analogous to a Ba2/BB rating in external agency terms. Understanding PD helps the CA appreciate the risk quantum they are underwriting.

**Scenario Probability Weighting:** For complex credits, the CA may assign probabilities to scenarios (base case 60%, upside 20%, downside 20%) and calculate a probability-weighted expected financial profile. This is particularly relevant for project finance or acquisition finance where outcomes are more binary.

## 5. Regulatory Framework

**Credit Policy:** The CA is responsible for ensuring that every credit request complies with the bank's credit policy. Non-compliant elements must be flagged as policy exceptions and separately approved. Common policy exceptions include: insufficient security cover, tenor beyond the policy maximum, loan-to-value above the policy maximum, or lending to an excluded sector without credit committee approval.

**Single Obligor Limits:** Regulatory capital rules and internal policy set maximum exposure limits to any single obligor or connected group. The CA must check total group exposure (including facilities at all group entities, contingent exposures from guarantees, and market risk from derivatives) against the single obligor limit before recommending approval.

**IFRS 9 Stage Classification:** The CA must assess the appropriate IFRS 9 stage for the facility: Stage 1 (no significant increase in credit risk since origination), Stage 2 (significant increase in credit risk, lifetime ECL), Stage 3 (credit-impaired). New facilities are booked at Stage 1 unless there is evidence of significant credit deterioration at inception.

**Anti-Money Laundering:** The CA must flag any features of the transaction that warrant AML scrutiny: unusual payment flows, complex ownership structures, transactions involving high-risk jurisdictions, unusually high cash usage in the business, or purpose that does not align with the business's stated activities.

**Data Protection:** Financial information provided by the borrower for credit assessment must be handled in accordance with UK GDPR. The CA should not share borrower financials outside the need-to-know group (credit team, legal team, external advisers under NDA).

## 6. Data Required

The CA works with the following information to produce a credit paper:

**From the RM:**
- Internal credit request form (RM's summary of the proposed facility, purpose, structure, and pricing)
- Client relationship context (history, cross-sell products, strategic importance)
- RM's preliminary financial commentary
- Any client-provided information already obtained

**From the Borrower (via RM):**
- Audited financial statements for the last three financial years (five years for larger credits)
- Most recent management accounts (no more than three months old at submission)
- Board-approved financial projections / business plan
- Details of any material post-balance-sheet events
- Ownership structure / corporate structure chart
- Details of existing indebtedness (copies of existing facility agreements or term sheets)

**From Internal Systems:**
- Group exposure summary (all existing facilities across all group entities)
- Internal rating history
- Covenant compliance history
- Account conduct history (excesses, late payments)
- Previous credit papers and approval decisions

**From External Sources:**
- Credit bureau reports (Dun & Bradstreet, Experian)
- Companies House filings (latest confirmation statement, accounts, charges register)
- Sector intelligence reports (bank's economists, industry bodies)
- Relevant press coverage

## 7. How Analysts Actually Work

**Receipt of Credit Request:** The CA receives a credit request from the RM, typically via the bank's workflow management system (or by email in smaller institutions). The CA first performs a completeness check — is all required information present? Are the financials current? Is the credit request form fully completed with purpose, structure, and proposed covenants?

**Incomplete Submissions:** The CA should not begin analysis on an incomplete submission. A formal deficiency note is sent to the RM listing missing items, with a revised deadline. Beginning analysis with incomplete information wastes time and produces unreliable conclusions.

**Spreading Financials:** The CA enters three to five years of financial data into the spreading template, making normalisation adjustments as described above. This process takes two to six hours depending on complexity. The CA checks internal consistency (balance sheet must balance; cash flow must bridge opening and closing cash) and flags any significant differences between reported and normalised figures.

**Business Risk Assessment:** Before running the numbers, the CA assesses business risk qualitatively. Key questions: What does this business do? How does it make money? Who are its customers and suppliers? What are the key risks to earnings? Is the industry growing or shrinking? What is the competitive position of this borrower? What are the key person dependencies?

**Financial Risk Assessment:** The CA then runs the quantitative analysis: trend analysis, ratio calculation (DSCR, leverage, interest cover, TNW, current ratio), sensitivity testing, and covenant adequacy testing.

**Writing the Credit Paper:** The credit paper follows a standard structure: executive summary, borrower background, management assessment, industry risk, financial analysis (historical and projected), facility structure review (is the structure appropriate for the purpose?), security analysis, covenant adequacy, IFRS 9 stage assessment, risk rating rationale, and recommendation.

**Challenging the RM:** A key part of the CA's job is to challenge RM assumptions. If the RM's credit request assumes 15% revenue growth but the historical trend is 3%, the CA must challenge this and use a more conservative projection. The CA should document the assumption used and explain the basis.

**Turnaround Times:** Typical internal service level agreements:
- Simple annual review (existing borrower, stable performance): 3–5 business days
- Complex annual review (deteriorated performance, covenant issues): 5–10 business days
- New facility (straightforward bilateral): 5–10 business days
- Complex new facility (acquisition finance, syndicated, leveraged): 15–25 business days

## 9. SQL Implementation

```sql
-- ===== CREDIT REQUEST TRACKING =====
SELECT
    cr.request_id,
    cr.client_name,
    cr.rm_name,
    cr.facility_amount,
    cr.request_type,          -- 'NEW_FACILITY', 'ANNUAL_REVIEW', 'AMENDMENT'
    cr.date_received,
    cr.date_assigned,
    cr.analyst_name,
    cr.target_completion,
    cr.actual_completion,
    DATEDIFF(DAY, cr.date_received, ISNULL(cr.actual_completion, GETDATE()))
        AS days_in_process,
    cr.status,                -- 'PENDING', 'IN_PROGRESS', 'SUBMITTED', 'APPROVED', 'DECLINED'
    CASE
        WHEN cr.actual_completion IS NULL
         AND GETDATE() > cr.target_completion THEN 'OVERDUE'
        WHEN cr.actual_completion IS NULL
         AND DATEDIFF(DAY, GETDATE(), cr.target_completion) <= 2 THEN 'AT RISK'
        ELSE 'ON TRACK'
    END AS sla_status
FROM credit_requests cr
WHERE cr.status IN ('PENDING', 'IN_PROGRESS')
ORDER BY cr.target_completion ASC;

-- ===== FINANCIAL SPREAD DATA STORAGE =====
-- credit_financials table: stores spreading output
SELECT
    cf.client_id,
    c.client_name,
    cf.financial_year,
    cf.reporting_standard,  -- 'IFRS', 'FRS102', 'US GAAP'
    cf.revenue,
    cf.gross_profit,
    cf.ebitda_reported,
    cf.ebitda_normalised,
    cf.ebitda_margin_normalised,
    cf.net_interest_expense,
    cf.tax_paid,
    cf.capex_total,
    cf.capex_maintenance,
    cf.operating_cash_flow,
    cf.free_cash_flow,
    cf.total_debt,
    cf.cash_and_equivalents,
    cf.net_debt,
    cf.tangible_net_worth,
    cf.total_equity,
    -- Derived ratios
    cf.ebitda_normalised / NULLIF(cf.net_interest_expense, 0) AS interest_cover,
    cf.net_debt / NULLIF(cf.ebitda_normalised, 0) AS leverage_x,
    (cf.operating_cash_flow - cf.capex_maintenance)
        / NULLIF(cf.net_interest_expense + cf.scheduled_debt_repayments, 0) AS dscr
FROM credit_financials cf
JOIN clients c ON cf.client_id = c.client_id
WHERE c.client_id = @client_id
ORDER BY cf.financial_year DESC;

-- ===== ANALYST WORKLOAD REPORT =====
SELECT
    analyst_name,
    COUNT(*) AS active_requests,
    SUM(CASE WHEN request_type = 'NEW_FACILITY' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS new_facilities,
    SUM(CASE WHEN request_type = 'ANNUAL_REVIEW' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS annual_reviews,
    SUM(CASE WHEN GETDATE() > target_completion THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS overdue,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(DAY, date_received, ISNULL(actual_completion, GETDATE()))) AS avg_days
FROM credit_requests
WHERE status IN ('PENDING', 'IN_PROGRESS')
GROUP BY analyst_name
ORDER BY active_requests DESC;

-- ===== CREDIT PAPER QUALITY METRICS =====
-- Track how often credit papers are returned for rework
SELECT
    cr.analyst_name,
    COUNT(DISTINCT cr.request_id) AS total_papers,
    COUNT(rw.rework_id) AS rework_count,
    ROUND(COUNT(rw.rework_id) * 100.0 / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT cr.request_id), 0), 1)
        AS rework_rate_pct,
    STRING_AGG(DISTINCT rw.rework_reason, '; ') AS common_rework_reasons
FROM credit_requests cr
LEFT JOIN credit_paper_reworks rw ON cr.request_id = rw.request_id
WHERE cr.actual_completion >= DATEADD(MONTH, -6, GETDATE())
GROUP BY cr.analyst_name
ORDER BY rework_rate_pct DESC;
```

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from typing import Optional, List, Dict
import warnings
warnings.filterwarnings('ignore')


# ===== FINANCIAL SPREADING MODEL =====

@dataclass
class FinancialYear:
    """Single year of financial data for a borrower."""
    year: int
    revenue: float
    gross_profit: float
    ebitda_reported: float
    exceptional_items: float          # positive = exceptional cost
    non_recurring_income: float       # positive = remove from normalised EBITDA
    da: float                         # D&A (should already be in EBITDA)
    net_interest: float               # interest expense net of income
    tax_paid: float                   # cash tax paid
    capex_total: float
    capex_maintenance: float
    operating_cash_flow: float        # per cash flow statement
    total_debt: float
    cash: float
    total_equity: float
    intangible_assets: float
    scheduled_debt_repayments: float

    @property
    def ebitda_normalised(self) -> float:
        return self.ebitda_reported + self.exceptional_items - self.non_recurring_income

    @property
    def ebitda_margin(self) -> float:
        return self.ebitda_normalised / self.revenue if self.revenue else 0

    @property
    def interest_cover(self) -> float:
        return self.ebitda_normalised / self.net_interest if self.net_interest else 0

    @property
    def net_debt(self) -> float:
        return self.total_debt - self.cash

    @property
    def leverage(self) -> float:
        return self.net_debt / self.ebitda_normalised if self.ebitda_normalised else 0

    @property
    def tangible_net_worth(self) -> float:
        return self.total_equity - self.intangible_assets

    @property
    def free_cash_flow(self) -> float:
        return self.operating_cash_flow - self.capex_total

    @property
    def dscr(self) -> float:
        numerator = (self.operating_cash_flow
                     - self.capex_maintenance
                     - self.tax_paid)
        denominator = self.net_interest + self.scheduled_debt_repayments
        return numerator / denominator if denominator else 0


class FinancialSpread:
    """Multi-year financial spread with trend analysis and sensitivity testing."""

    def __init__(self, borrower_name: str, years: List[FinancialYear]):
        self.borrower_name = borrower_name
        self.years = sorted(years, key=lambda y: y.year)

    def summary_table(self) -> pd.DataFrame:
        rows = []
        for fy in self.years:
            rows.append({
                'Year': fy.year,
                'Revenue (£m)': round(fy.revenue, 2),
                'Norm EBITDA (£m)': round(fy.ebitda_normalised, 2),
                'EBITDA Margin %': round(fy.ebitda_margin * 100, 1),
                'Interest Cover (x)': round(fy.interest_cover, 2),
                'Net Debt (£m)': round(fy.net_debt, 2),
                'Leverage (x)': round(fy.leverage, 2),
                'DSCR (x)': round(fy.dscr, 2),
                'TNW (£m)': round(fy.tangible_net_worth, 2),
                'FCF (£m)': round(fy.free_cash_flow, 2)
            })
        return pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index('Year')

    def revenue_cagr(self) -> float:
        if len(self.years) < 2:
            return 0.0
        start, end = self.years[0].revenue, self.years[-1].revenue
        n = len(self.years) - 1
        return (end / start) ** (1 / n) - 1

    def sensitivity_dscr(
        self,
        base_year_idx: int = -1,
        revenue_shocks: List[float] = None,
        margin_shocks: List[float] = None
    ) -> pd.DataFrame:
        """Stress test DSCR across revenue and margin scenarios."""
        if revenue_shocks is None:
            revenue_shocks = [-0.30, -0.20, -0.10, 0.0, 0.10]
        if margin_shocks is None:
            margin_shocks = [-0.03, -0.02, -0.01, 0.0, 0.01]

        base = self.years[base_year_idx]
        results = []
        for rev_shock in revenue_shocks:
            row = {'Revenue Shock': f'{rev_shock:+.0%}'}
            for margin_shock in margin_shocks:
                stressed_revenue = base.revenue * (1 + rev_shock)
                stressed_ebitda = stressed_revenue * (base.ebitda_margin + margin_shock)
                stressed_ocf = stressed_ebitda - base.tax_paid
                dscr = ((stressed_ocf - base.capex_maintenance)
                        / (base.net_interest + base.scheduled_debt_repayments))
                row[f'Margin {margin_shock:+.0%}'] = round(dscr, 2)
            results.append(row)
        return pd.DataFrame(results).set_index('Revenue Shock')

    def covenant_compliance_check(
        self,
        min_dscr: float = 1.25,
        max_leverage: float = 3.5,
        min_interest_cover: float = 2.5
    ) -> pd.DataFrame:
        rows = []
        for fy in self.years:
            rows.append({
                'Year': fy.year,
                'DSCR': round(fy.dscr, 2),
                'DSCR OK': 'PASS' if fy.dscr >= min_dscr else 'FAIL',
                'Leverage': round(fy.leverage, 2),
                'Leverage OK': 'PASS' if fy.leverage <= max_leverage else 'FAIL',
                'Int Cover': round(fy.interest_cover, 2),
                'Int Cover OK': 'PASS' if fy.interest_cover >= min_interest_cover else 'FAIL'
            })
        return pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index('Year')

            self.status[item] = True

    def summary(self) -> pd.DataFrame:
        rows = []
        for category, item in self.CHECKLIST:
            rows.append({
                'Category': category,
                'Checklist Item': item,
                'Status': 'DONE' if self.status[item] else 'PENDING'
            })
        df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
        complete = df['Status'].eq('DONE').sum()
        total = len(df)
        print(f"Completion: {complete}/{total} ({complete/total*100:.0f}%)")
        return df

    def incomplete_items(self) -> List[str]:
        return [item for item, done in self.status.items() if not done]
```

## 11. Interview Questions

**Q1: A borrower's accounts show strong EBITDA growth but the cash flow statement shows negative operating cash flow. How do you interpret this?**
A: This is a working capital trap. EBITDA growth is real but the cash is being consumed by working capital build — rising debtors, increasing inventory, or falling creditors. I would calculate the cash conversion cycle and investigate each working capital component. This does not necessarily mean the credit is bad, but it means the borrower needs more liquidity support, and the RCF must be sized for peak working capital, not average utilisation.

**Q2: What are the key differences between an annual review and a new facility credit paper?**
A: An annual review is a periodic reaffirmation that the existing facility remains appropriate given the borrower's current financial condition. It focuses on performance vs forecast, covenant compliance, and any changes in risk profile. A new facility paper also requires analysis of the new structure (purpose, tenor, pricing, security), whether the new debt quantum is appropriate, and what changes in the borrower's obligations arise from the new facility.

**Q3: The RM's credit request assumes 20% revenue growth over the next three years for a borrower in a mature, low-growth industry. How do you handle this?**
A: I challenge the assumption directly. I research industry growth rates from sector publications, compare to the borrower's own historical growth (which may be 3–5% per annum), and then run the credit analysis on both the RM's case and a more conservative case. The credit paper presents both, explains the basis for my chosen base case, and stress-tests against the downside. I document the RM's assumption and my departure from it clearly.

**Q4: What is a normalisation adjustment and when is it appropriate?**
A: A normalisation adjustment removes items from EBITDA that are not representative of the ongoing trading performance of the business. Appropriate adjustments include: one-off restructuring costs, acquisition integration costs, litigation settlements, and non-recurring advisory fees. Inappropriate adjustments are those that mask recurring costs — for example, treating stock write-downs as exceptional when inventory management is structurally poor. Every adjustment must be documented and justified.

**Q5: You receive a credit request with management accounts that are six months old. What do you do?**
A: The accounts are too stale. Bank policy typically requires management accounts no more than three months old. I send a deficiency note to the RM requesting current management accounts before I begin analysis. If the RM explains that the client is unresponsive, this itself is an early warning signal that I document in the credit paper.

## 12. Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Starting analysis with incomplete information.** A common mistake is to begin spreading financials while still waiting for management accounts or security information. The analysis then has to be redone when the missing information arrives, wasting time and potentially producing inconsistent drafts.

**Mistake 2: Copying the RM's narrative without independent verification.** A credit paper that simply echoes the RM's view is not independent analysis. The CA must independently assess the business model, management quality, and market position — not just relay what the RM told them.

**Mistake 3: Using reported EBITDA without normalisation.** Reported EBITDA from the accounts may be inflated by one-off items. A CA who uses reported EBITDA without adjustment overstates debt service capacity.

**Mistake 4: Failing to read the notes to the accounts.** Key disclosures — contingent liabilities, pension deficit, related party transactions, going concern doubt, post-balance-sheet events — are in the notes. A CA who only reads the face of the financial statements misses these.

**Mistake 5: Presenting single-year ratios as if they are trends.** A single-year DSCR of 1.5x tells you little if it was 2.0x the year before and is forecast to be 1.1x next year. Trend matters more than snapshot.

**Mistake 6: Setting covenants with too little headroom.** If a borrower just passes a 1.25x DSCR covenant in the base case with no headroom, any minor underperformance triggers a technical breach. Covenants should be set so the borrower has meaningful headroom in the base case and breaches only in a material downside.

## 13. Case Studies

**Case Study 1: The EBITDA Add-Back Abuse**
An RM submits a credit request for a facilities management company citing EBITDA of £8m. The CA spreads the accounts and finds that management has included £2.5m of add-backs in their adjusted EBITDA figure: £1.2m for "restructuring", £0.8m for "integration costs" (the company made an acquisition two years ago), and £0.5m for "one-off legal costs". The CA investigates: the restructuring has occurred in every year for five years (not one-off), the integration costs are two years post-acquisition (no longer exceptional), and the legal costs relate to recurring litigation with a subcontractor. The CA strips all three add-backs, reducing normalised EBITDA to £5.5m. Leverage changes from 2.5x (using management's EBITDA) to 4.5x (using CA's normalised EBITDA). The credit paper recommends a reduced facility size.

**Case Study 2: The Hidden Pension Deficit**
A manufacturing borrower has a defined benefit pension scheme. The P&L shows a pension credit of £0.3m (positive variance in actuarial assumptions). The CA reads Note 18 to the accounts and discovers a pension deficit of £12m, with agreed deficit reduction contributions of £1.5m per annum. These contributions are cash outflows that do not appear in the EBITDA or the normalised earnings calculation but must be deducted from free cash flow for DSCR purposes. Adjusting for pension contributions reduces DSCR from 1.6x to 1.1x — below the proposed covenant threshold.

**Case Study 3: The Group Structure Problem**
An RM submits a credit request for the topco of a group of three operating companies. The topco has no direct operations — it holds shares in the three opcos. The CA reviews the group structure chart and discovers: the opcos are in different jurisdictions (UK, Germany, Netherlands), dividend upstreaming from the German and Dutch entities requires local board approval and is subject to local solvency tests, and there are minority shareholders in two of the three opcos who could block distributions. The CA structures the credit paper to require guarantees from all three opcos and cross-default clauses. The credit is recommended on a more conservative structure than the RM originally proposed.

## 14. Iterative Reinforcement

**Week 1 — Spreading Practice:** Obtain three years of published accounts from a UK-listed company. Spread the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement into the bank's standard template. Calculate all key ratios. Compare your normalised EBITDA to analysts' consensus estimates.

**Week 2 — Covenant Analysis:** For the same company, determine what financial covenants would be appropriate if the bank were lending 3.0x EBITDA. Set covenant thresholds, calculate current headroom, and run sensitivity analysis to determine what level of EBITDA decline would trigger a covenant breach.

**Week 3 — Credit Paper Drafting:** Draft a one-page executive summary for the credit you have analysed. The summary should contain: borrower overview, facility request summary, three key strengths, three key risks, and a clear recommendation.

**Week 4 — Challenge Simulation:** Take a credit paper draft and role-play as the Credit Manager. Write five challenge questions that a rigorous Credit Manager should ask about the paper. Then answer each question with reference to the analysis.

**Self-Assessment Questions:**
- Can you explain three normalisation adjustments and when each is/is not appropriate?
- Can you describe the difference between EBITDA-based coverage and cash flow-based DSCR?
- Can you construct a two-way sensitivity table for DSCR and explain what it tells you?
- Can you identify what items in the notes to accounts most affect your credit assessment?
