1 — Python, SQL and Data Engineering Foundations

## Live Scenario
A newly-hired data engineer at a transaction-banking platform spends their first two weeks writing technically excellent PySpark code for a customer-transaction ingestion pipeline -- then discovers in sprint review that the Business Analyst had already flagged, three weeks earlier, that the source system was being decommissioned in favor of a different one, a fact that would have changed the entire pipeline design. The code itself was not wrong; the engineer had simply never asked who else already knew something relevant before starting to build.

## The Question
> Identify the stakeholders a data engineer should routinely check in with before and during a pipeline build (not just at planning and sign-off), and explain what specific risk each check-in is meant to catch -- using the scenario's decommissioned-source-system failure as the concrete case to test the answer against.

## Solution Code
```text
Stakeholder check-ins for a data engineering build, and the specific risk
each one catches (not a generic list -- each entry is justified against a
real failure mode):

- **Business Analysts / Product Owners** -- own the requirements and the
  business context around *why* a data source or pipeline is needed. In
  the scenario, the BA already knew the source system was being
  decommissioned; a short check-in before design would have surfaced it.
  Risk caught: building against a requirement that has already changed.

- **Data Architects** -- own the target-state data platform design and
  cross-pipeline consistency. A pipeline built without an architect
  check-in can duplicate an existing ingestion path, or violate a
  platform convention (naming, partitioning strategy, schema registry
  usage) that only becomes visible when two pipelines collide later.
  Risk caught: architectural drift that is expensive to unwind once live.

- **Data Scientists / Analytics consumers** -- the people who will
  actually query the output. A schema or grain decision that looks
  reasonable to the engineer can be unusable for the downstream model or
  report it was built for, if the consumer was never consulted on what
  shape they actually need.
  Risk caught: a technically working pipeline nobody can use as-is.

- **Source-system owners** -- the team operating the system being
  ingested from. They know about planned decommissions, schema changes,
  maintenance windows and data-quality issues in their own system before
  anyone downstream does.
  Risk caught: exactly the scenario's failure -- building against a
  source that is about to disappear or change shape.

- **Operations / on-call** -- the team who will be paged when the
  pipeline breaks at 2am. A pipeline designed without their input often
  lacks the observability (Chapter 5) or resilience (Chapter 6) they
  need to actually operate it.
  Risk caught: a pipeline that works in testing but is unsupportable in
  production.

Agile/Scrum mechanics -- sprint planning, backlog grooming, daily
stand-ups, retrospectives -- are the *structured opportunities* where
these check-ins are supposed to happen by default, not a separate
activity layered on top. The scenario's failure is specifically a
stand-up/planning failure: the information existed and was said out loud
three weeks earlier, but the engineer building this specific pipeline
was not in the room, or the connection to their work was not made
explicit.
```

## Expected Output
Applying this to the scenario directly: a source-system-owner check-in during sprint planning -- one specific question, "is anything changing about this source system in the near term?" -- would have surfaced the decommissioning before any code was written, at the cost of a single planning-stage conversation instead of two weeks of rework.

## Explanation
**This lesson deliberately does not teach Agile/Scrum ceremonies as a checklist to perform** -- it teaches what specific risk each stakeholder relationship is meant to catch, because a ceremony attended without that understanding (sitting silently in a stand-up) provides none of its actual value.

**The scenario is a genuine, common failure mode in real data engineering teams**, not a contrived teaching example: technically strong engineers who default to solo building are exactly the profile most likely to skip the low-status, unglamorous work of checking in with a BA or a source-system owner before starting — and it is exactly this kind of miscommunication, not a coding mistake, that produces the most expensive rework in real platform teams.

**Important scope honesty for this lesson, and for this entire chapter:** completing these six Chapter 1 lessons establishes foundational discipline -- typed Python, sargable SQL, star-schema modelling, resilient API integration, PR-based version control, and stakeholder awareness. It does **not**, on its own, constitute the 8-10 years of enterprise data engineering experience a senior PySpark/Cloudera role like this track's source posting actually requires. The distributed-systems, Hadoop-ecosystem, and production-operations skills that posting is built around start in Chapter 2 and continue through Chapter 6 -- this chapter is the foundation those chapters are built on, not a substitute for them.
