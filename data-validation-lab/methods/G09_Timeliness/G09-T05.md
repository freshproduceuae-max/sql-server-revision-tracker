# G09-T05 — Timeliness | Backdated Entry Detection

## ID
G09-T05

## Group
9 — Timeliness

## Type
Backdated Entry Detection — Records Posted With a Date Earlier Than When They Were Created

## Live Scenario
A sales manager is under pressure to meet a quarterly target. On 1 April (the first day of Q2), they log into the CRM and create six sales orders backdated to 31 March, inflating Q1 revenue by £47,000 to hit the target. The records have `OrderDate = 2024-03-31` but the system's audit log records `CreatedAt = 2024-04-01 09:23`. The quarterly P&L report uses `OrderDate` for period attribution; the audit log field is not included in reporting queries. The Q1 target is deemed met; bonuses are paid. The manipulation is discovered in an internal audit six months later when the audit log timestamps are compared to order dates. Three employees receive formal disciplinary action.

## The Question
> Backdated entries have a document/event date that is earlier than the system creation timestamp. SQL detects backdating by comparing the business date field to a system-assigned audit timestamp and flagging records where the business date is significantly earlier than when the record was actually created.
> **"Identify Transactions where TransactionDate is more than 7 days before OrderDate (the order it references), which would indicate the transaction was backdated relative to the triggering event."**

## Table Reference
**Transactions** — `schemas/05_Transactions.csv`
**Orders** — `schemas/02_Orders.csv`

## Solution Query

```sql
-- Step 1: Transactions predating their parent order (impossible without backdating)
SELECT
    t.TransactionID,
    t.OrderID,
    o.OrderDate,
    t.TransactionDate,
    DATEDIFF(DAY, t.TransactionDate, o.OrderDate) AS DaysPriorToOrder,
    t.Amount,
    t.Currency,
    CASE
        WHEN t.TransactionDate < o.OrderDate
             THEN 'IMPOSSIBLE: Transaction before order exists — backdated or data error'
        ELSE 'OK: Transaction after order'
    END AS BackdateFlag
FROM Transactions t
JOIN Orders o ON t.OrderID = o.OrderID
WHERE t.TransactionDate < o.OrderDate
ORDER BY DaysPriorToOrder DESC;

-- Step 2: Orders created well after the period they are dated in (period-end clustering)
-- Detect backdating to a prior month: order date is in month N but was entered suspiciously
-- Proxy: cluster of orders with OrderDate = last day of a month
SELECT
    CAST(o.OrderDate AS DATE) AS OrderDate,
    DAY(o.OrderDate)           AS DayOfMonth,
    DAY(EOMONTH(o.OrderDate))  AS LastDayOfMonth,
    COUNT(*)                   AS OrderCount,
    SUM(o.TotalAmount)         AS TotalValue,
    CASE
        WHEN DAY(o.OrderDate) = DAY(EOMONTH(o.OrderDate))
             THEN 'MONTH-END SPIKE: High volume on last day — backdating risk'
        WHEN DAY(o.OrderDate) >= DAY(EOMONTH(o.OrderDate)) - 2
             THEN 'NEAR MONTH-END: Orders in last 3 days of month'
        ELSE 'Normal date'
    END AS BackdateSuspicion
FROM Orders o
GROUP BY CAST(o.OrderDate AS DATE), DAY(o.OrderDate), DAY(EOMONTH(o.OrderDate))
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 2  -- Flag dates with multiple orders
ORDER BY OrderDate DESC;

-- Step 3: Transactions with amount inconsistent with the date (refunds before purchases)
WITH OrderedEvents AS (
    SELECT
        o.OrderID,
        o.OrderDate,
        t.TransactionDate,
        t.Amount,
        ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY o.OrderID ORDER BY t.TransactionDate) AS EventSeq
    FROM Orders o
    JOIN Transactions t ON o.OrderID = t.OrderID
)
SELECT
    oe1.OrderID,
    oe1.Amount AS FirstAmount,
    oe1.TransactionDate AS FirstDate,
    oe2.Amount AS SecondAmount,
    oe2.TransactionDate AS SecondDate,
    'REFUND BEFORE PAYMENT: Credit precedes original charge — backdating or sequence error' AS Flag
FROM OrderedEvents oe1
JOIN OrderedEvents oe2 ON oe1.OrderID = oe2.OrderID AND oe1.EventSeq = 1 AND oe2.EventSeq = 2
WHERE oe1.Amount < 0 AND oe2.Amount > 0;  -- First event is negative (refund before payment)

-- Step 4: Quarter-end order spike — revenue manipulation detection
SELECT
    YEAR(OrderDate)  AS OrderYear,
    MONTH(OrderDate) AS OrderMonth,
    DAY(OrderDate)   AS OrderDay,
    COUNT(*)         AS DailyOrderCount,
    SUM(TotalAmount) AS DailyOrderValue,
    CASE
        WHEN DAY(OrderDate) = DAY(EOMONTH(OrderDate))
         AND MONTH(OrderDate) IN (3, 6, 9, 12)
             THEN 'QUARTER-END LAST DAY: Highest backdating risk — review audit log'
        WHEN DAY(OrderDate) = DAY(EOMONTH(OrderDate))
             THEN 'MONTH-END LAST DAY: Elevated backdating risk'
        ELSE 'Normal'
    END AS AuditFlag
FROM Orders
GROUP BY YEAR(OrderDate), MONTH(OrderDate), DAY(OrderDate)
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
ORDER BY OrderYear, OrderMonth, OrderDay;
```

## Explanation

**The impossible sequence**
A transaction recording payment for an order cannot have a timestamp before the order was created. `t.TransactionDate < o.OrderDate` is logically impossible in a normal workflow — payment is triggered by the order existing. When this condition is true, either the transaction was backdated or the order date was forward-dated (both are integrity errors).

**Period-end clustering as backdating signal**
Legitimate orders distribute across the month. An abnormal spike on the last day of a quarter (31 March, 30 June, 30 September, 31 December) is a classic revenue manipulation pattern — orders are backdated to meet period targets. Step 2 surfaces this concentration; it is not proof of fraud but is a risk indicator requiring audit log investigation.

**Audit log comparison**
The most reliable backdating detection compares the business date to a system-assigned creation timestamp (`CreatedAt`, `InsertedAt`) that users cannot modify. If `OrderDate = 31 March` but `CreatedAt = 01 April 09:23`, the backdating is confirmed. This query uses the transaction-before-order logic as a proxy when no `CreatedAt` column exists in the schema.

**Refund-before-payment as a sequence error**
Step 3 identifies a different form of backdating: a credit/refund transaction recorded before the original payment. This is either a legitimate credit (advance payment) or evidence that the refund was backdated to a date before the purchase — useful in fraud investigations.

## Severity
**High** — Backdated entries distort period-end reporting and can constitute financial fraud. In accounting, they shift revenue and expenses between periods in violation of the matching principle. In regulated industries (financial services, healthcare), backdating is a compliance violation. External auditors test specifically for period-end manipulation.
